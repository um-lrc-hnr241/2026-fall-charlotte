Fruit 02 · Build 2

Individual paragraph

In Paradise Lost, Eve’s decision to eat the fruit allows us to question whether conscious thought actually causes physical action. After reasoning over and over about the fruit, Milton abruptly describes her actions: “she plucked, she eat”. The simplicity of these verbs contrasts with the complexity of Eve’s preceding thoughts, making the transition from her consciousness to physical action seem almost immediate. Her hand is described as “rash”, complicating the idea that her action comes from the reasoning she did in her head. This connects to Dolores in Westworld, who becomes more aware of her own thoughts and eventually acts against Ford. Her growing consciousness seems to give her more control over her actions, but the show also reveals that her actions are shaped by her programming. Eve and Dolores therefore both raise the question of whether being conscious actually gives one control over what they do, or if consciousness simply exists alongside external forces that guide one’s actions.

Fruit 03 · Build 3

Build Three

In Paradise Lost, Milton portrays reflexivity as a quality of consciousness that allows a person to examine their own thoughts and desires. Eve’s reasoning before and after eating the fruit illustrates that consciousness can do much more than simply come before an action. It can change how a person understands what they want and how they respond to it afterwards. This moves the focus from asking whether consciousness controls an action to asking what impact consciousness has on that action. At the same time, Milton leaves open whether becoming aware of one’s own thoughts actually gives a person greater control over what they do.

Before eating the fruit, Eve investigates why she wants it. She considers the fruit “of virtue to make wise” and asks, “What hinders then / To reach and feed at once both body and mind?” (Milton 778-780). Eve is aware of her desire for the fruit and begins questioning whether that desire should be resisted. Yet the question, “What hinders then” does something more complicated than merely asking whether she should eat or not. The question shows that she is not just thinking about the fruit itself, but also trying to decide whether her desire for it is a good enough reason to act. Because she can’t find a reason to stop herself, eating the fruit begins to seem like the reasonable choice. Her thinking therefore impacts what she does next.

This becomes even clearer after Eve eats the fruit. Instead of focusing only on what the fruit tastes like or what it has physically done to her, Eve begins looking into how this experience may change her relationship with Adam. She asks, “But to Adam in what sort / Shall I appear” (Milton 816-17). She considers whether she should tell Adam what happened, whether she might become “Superior” and asks “for inferior who is free?” Eve is now thinking about herself, her relationship with Adam, and what her new knowledge means for her. This shows another side of reflexivity. Eve is able to step back from what she has experienced and think about what it means for her. At the same time, her thoughts are still connected to her desire for knowledge and her concern about Adam. Becoming aware of her thoughts does not remove those influences.

Eve later on describes the fruit as having “opener” her eyes with “dilated spirits” and an “ampler heart” (Milton 874-875). Eve believes that the fruit has changed the way she sees and understands the world. She is aware that something has changed within her, which illustrates her thinking about her own mental state. However, this new awareness does not inherently mean that she can fully control her actions. Her consciousness impacts how she understands what has happened, but her desires continue to shape that perspective.

Eve’s experience illustrates that consciousness can impact what a person does without entirely controlling their actions. Eve thinks about what she wants, gives herself reasons to act, and considers what her actions mean. However, her thoughts are still influenced by her desires and the situation around her. Milton therefore shows that being aware of one’s own thoughts can change how you act, even if it does not give you complete control of your actions.

Fruit 04 · Build 4

Build Four

One might object that reflexivity does not fully fit Milton’s representation of consciousness in Eve’s decision to eat the fruit because her reasoning does not necessarily illustrate an awareness of how her own thoughts influence her choices. Rather than critically examining her desire, Eve seems to create arguments that make acting on that desire appear more reasonable. When she considers the fruit’s potential to make her wise, describing it as “of virtue to make wise,” (Paradise Lost, 774-775) she interprets its forbidden nature as a possible source of knowledge instead of a reason to avoid it. Rather than questioning why she is attracted to the fruit or considering whether her desire might be misleading her, she focuses on the potential benefits of eating it. Instead of asking whether she should eat the fruit, Eve appears to ask why she should not. Her reasoning in this matter could therefore suggest that she is not truly reflecting on her own consciousness but instead rationalizing a decision she already wants to make. If reflexivity requires a person to examine their own thinking critically, Eve reasoning might seem to demonstrate its absence rather than its presence.

However, this objection assumes that reflexivity must involve evaluating and controlling one’s thoughts rather than simply becoming aware of them and interpreting them. Eve’s reasoning illustrates that she does more than experience a desire for the fruit. She actively considers what eating it might mean for her knowledge and identity. By describing the fruit as something to make her wiser, her mind develops an interpretation of its value that allows her to see eating it as an opportunity for intellectual improvement. Her point about feeding both body and mind also shows that she is considering the action in relation to different parts of herself. She does not choose to simply reach for the fruit without thinking, she ensures to give the possibility of eating it a significance that makes the action appear worthwhile. Although, her reasoning is biased by what she already desires in her heart. Reflexivity in this sense, is more about her ability to consider her own motivations and interpret what those motivations mean.

This suggests that reflexivity can influence a person’s decisions without making those decisions more rational or controlled. Eve’s reflection does not protect her from temptation. It actually gives her a way to perceive her temptation as something beneficial to her. This complicates the idea that self-awareness leads to better judgment. A person may recognize what they want and develop a rational argument for pursuing it without even realizing how their desire shapes their reasoning for doing so. Eve’s interpretation of the fruit enables her to present the act of eating it as a way to improve herself, even though this interpretation moves her towards disobedience. Her consciousness is therefore not just a force that helps her distinguish right and wrong. It also helps her create explanations through which she understands her motivations and justifies her choices.

Milton’s portrayal of Eve ultimately suggests that reflexivity can reveal and bias an individual’s relationship with their own desires. Even though Eve’s reasoning might appear to be a form of rationalization rather than genuine reflection, the two processes don’t have to be mutually exclusive. She can analyze the meaning of her desire while also interpreting it in a way that ultimately supports the action she wants to take. Reflexivity therefore does not guarantee that individuals will gain control over their impulses and temptations simply by contemplating them. Instead, it can actually make those desires more persuasive by giving them a rational justification. Eve’s decision, in this way, demonstrates that consciousness is more than just about experiencing desires, but also interpreting those desires, and behaving subsequently.

Fruit 05 · Build 5

Build Five

In Paradise Lost and the Bhagavad Gita, reflexivity helps explore the relationship between an individual’s understanding of themselves and their ability to control their actions. Both readings consider how desires influence the way individuals interpret their experiences and make decisions. However, they approach self-understanding differently. Milton presents Eve’s reasoning as an example of how personal desires can shape an individual’s interpretation of a situation, while the Bhagavad Gita explores how recognizing the forces influencing one’s judgment can help an individual acquire greater control over their actions. Both readings suggest that consciousness does not necessarily gain control just by looking inwards into one’s thoughts. Instead, they demonstrate that reflexivity requires recognizing how one’s own perspective influences the conclusions one reaches.

In Paradise Lost, Eve’s consideration of the forbidden fruit demonstrates the difference between thinking about a desire and actually examining how that desire influences one’s judgment. Before eating the fruit, Eve describes it as being “of virtue to make wise”. She then asks, “What hinders then / To reach and feed at once both body and mind” (IX 778-779). Eve is not just responding to the fruit without thinking first. She considers what it may offer her and interprets eating it as a way for her to become wiser. However, her reasoning is shaped by her desire for the knowledge she believes the fruit can provide. This shows that even though Eve examines the possible benefits of eating the fruit, she doesn’t actually seem to investigate how her own desire for wisdom biases her interpretation. Her reasoning therefore risks becoming rationalization rather than genuine reflexivity. She develops an explanation that supports what she already wants instead of questioning whether her desire is influencing her judgment. Milton’s portrayal complicates the relationship between consciousness and self-understanding because her ability to reason is not necessarily evidence that she understands herself or what exactly is shaping her decision.

The Bhagavad Gita approaches this problem through Krishna’s instructions to Arjuna. In the Third Teaching, Krishna explains that desire can destroy knowledge and judgment. He tells Arjuna to control his senses and describes a hierarchy in which understanding is superior to the mind, while the self is higher than understanding. This hierarchy is critical because it indicates that an individual’s immediate thoughts are not their highest source of understanding. By placing the self above understanding, Krishna asks Arjuna to recognize the forces that influence his judgment rather than simply accepting his desires as reliable guides. This is different from Eve’s reasoning. While Eve considers the fruit through the benefits she desires, Krishna emphasizes the need to recognize how desire itself can distort understanding. The readings offer an interesting concept about how individuals must examine not only what they think, but also what influences the way they think.

The difference becomes clearer when Krishna explains that people are often mistaken about the causes of their own actions. In the Third Teaching, he states that action results from the qualities of nature, yet people influenced by individuality believe that they are the actor. This challenges the idea that individuals fully understand or control the forces behind their behavior, and it also makes the idea that reflexivity automatically produces freedom more nuanced. Even when someone examines their decisions, they may not recognize the deeper influences shaping them. Eve’s reasoning illustrates how desire can become part of the process through which a person justifies an action. Krishna’s teaching, on the other hand, encourages Arjuna to question his assumption that he is acting independently of the forces that shape and guide him. The two readings therefore approach reflexivity from different directions. Milton reveals to the readers how an individual’s perspective can limit self-awareness, while the Bhagavad Gita emphasizes the importance of recognizing those forces that influence action.

Milton and the Bhagavad Gita both use reflexivity in order to explore how recognizing the influences behind one’s thoughts and desires might change the way one acts. Yet, how can reflexivity allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality and influence its course? Reflexivity may make this possible because it allows individuals to look into their immediate impulses without responding to them right away. When someone recognizes a desire is influencing their judgment, they can consider an alternative action and imagine what might happen if they act differently. This creates the possibility of changing the course of events that happen afterwards before an action takes place. Eve demonstrates the limits of this possibility because her reasoning does not fully expose the influence her desire has on her judgment. Krishna’s instructions to Arjuna suggest that recognizing and monitoring these influences may create greater control over action. It is clear from both readings that understanding how one’s own perspective shapes one’s judgement is an important step towards gaining more influence and control over one’s actions.
