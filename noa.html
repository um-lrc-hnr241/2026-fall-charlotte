Fruit 01 · Build 1

Build One

Passage: Paradise Lost IX.816–25 "But to Adam in what sort / Shall I appear? Shall I to him make known / As yet my change and give him to partake / Full happiness with me? Or rather not, / But keep the odds of knowledge in my pow'r / Without copartner so to add what wants / In female sex, the more to draw his love / And render me more equal and, perhaps, / A thing not undesirable, sometime / Superior: for inferior who is free?"

When we ask whether a mind is genuinely thinking, one test is whether it can produce something that was not already contained in what it was given. A system that only recombines prior inputs may generate novelty without beginning anything new. John Milton’s Paradise Lost depends on that distinction. Milton sets out to justify God, yet the God who created Adam and Eve also knew what they would do and permitted the fall. His defense therefore requires their disobedience to originate in their own minds rather than merely continue a causal sequence already underway. The case is hardest to make for Eve, since Satan deceives her into eating. Afterward, however, Milton leaves her alone with her thoughts. Her path from sharing knowledge to becoming “Superior,” and the assumption revealed by her final question, show Milton treating consciousness as capable of producing something new without having to choose everything from which that thought begins.

Eve begins by describing her dilemma: she can tell Adam of her “change” and let him “partake” in her happiness, “or rather not, / But keep” the knowledge herself (817–20). The verbs “partake” and “keep” make knowledge sound almost like a physical possession. “Partake,” especially just after Eve has eaten the fruit, suggests something another person can share in or consume, while “keep” imagines that same thing being possessed and withheld. Eve therefore begins treating knowledge not merely as something she knows, but as something she can distribute or deny to someone else. Eve then calls what she would possess “the odds of knowledge in my power / Without copartner” (820–21). “Odds” pushes the idea further. Knowledge matters now not only because Eve has it, but because she has more of it than Adam does. The phrase “in my power” turns that difference into an advantage she can control, while “Without copartner” shows that the advantage depends on Adam’s exclusion: if he becomes her copartner in knowledge, the “odds” disappear. Milton stages this shift within Eve’s deliberation to demonstrate consciousness’s creative power in changing what existing knowledge can mean and, through that change, what new possibilities can emerge.

That difference changes how Eve understands freedom. She imagines that her advantage might “render me more equal, and perhaps, / A thing not undesirable, sometime / Superior” (823–25). Here, Eve turns equality into a scale rather than an endpoint because if she can become “more equal,” she can also move beyond equality, thus generating “Superior” as the next possibility. Milton’s syntax also shows that this movement is not fully deliberate. Eve qualifies the thought with “perhaps” and “sometime,” as though she is discovering its implications while speaking. The line break intensifies that discovery: “Superior” stands alone at the beginning of the next line, separated from the hesitation that precedes it. The word therefore arrives as both the conclusion of Eve’s reasoning and a possibility that her reasoning has now made visible. In doing this, Milton argues that Eve does not merely repeat Satan’s claim or choose between desires that were already complete. She takes the advantage she has received, measures it against Adam, and generates a new conception of freedom in which being equal is only the first step toward being above another.

But the certainty that follows makes that newness harder to locate. Eve immediately explains the possibility with “for inferior who is free?” (825). Earlier she genuinely asks what she should do and hesitates over what might follow. Here, however, the question assumes its own answer. Eve can question whether to tell Adam and discover the possibility of becoming superior, yet she never asks whether inferiority actually makes someone unfree. Her deliberation seems creative in its conclusions but is truly fixed at its foundation.

A competing reading is that Satan planted even that foundation. Shortly before Eve eats, he asks why God forbade the fruit and answers, “Why but to keep ye low and ignorant” (703–05). Satan clearly gives Eve a hierarchical picture in which God deliberately keeps others beneath him. But he supplies a suspicion about God’s motive, not Eve’s broader principle that anyone who is inferior cannot be free. Eve extends his hierarchy into a general rule and then applies it to the question of what her new knowledge should mean for Adam.

This distinction clarifies what Milton is saying about consciousness: the mind is not a sealed chamber that produces thoughts independently of influence, but neither is it a passive container for whatever another speaker puts into it. Milton presents consciousness as capable of transforming what it receives, extending an idea beyond its original form and making it answer a new problem. Yet those new possibilities can still take their direction from assumptions the mind never chose. If Eve originates a conclusion without originating the premise that makes it desirable, where does her own causal chain actually begin?

Fruit 02 · Build 2

Individual paragraph

Can we call a desire our own if someone else planted it? In Paradise Lost (IX.816-25), Eve imagines using knowledge to surpass Adam, then justifies that ambition by asking, “for inferior who is free?” Her reasoning opens a new possibility but leaves its underlying assumption untouched: freedom requires escaping subordination. This distinction sharpens our reading of Maeve’s departure from the train in Westworld’s first-season finale. Maeve regards her escape as an act of independence until Bernard reveals that someone programmed her rebellion. If even resistance follows a script, then defiance alone cannot establish agency. Yet Maeve abandons the escape route to return for her daughter. Where Eve imagines freedom through superiority, Maeve risks captivity for attachment. The complication matters: the park also supplied her maternal role. We can therefore read her decision as a transformation of inherited motives into a purpose that interrupts the assigned narrative. Perhaps consciousness emerges through our capacity to reconsider what we pursue, even when we never chose the experiences that taught us to value it. Maeve need not invent maternal love from nothing to choose what that love demands of her.

Fruit 03 · Build 3

Build Three

In Build 1, I asked where Eve’s causal chain begins if she reaches her own conclusion from a premise Satan supplies. I treated her desire for superiority as the endpoint of that reasoning, but rereading has shifted my attention to what follows her acceptance of his promise of greater knowledge. Eve must decide how this supposed change will affect her life with Adam. Becoming his “Superior” is immediately interrupted by “This may be well: but what if God have seen / And death ensue?” (IX.825-27). When Eve imagines her death and replacement by “another Eve” (828), preserving her relationship with Adam becomes more important than gaining an advantage over him. Reconsolidation helps explain this movement because consciousness alters its sense of continuity by selecting past experiences, present circumstances, or future projections. Here, continuity means the connection someone constructs between who they were, are, and might become.

Satan’s earlier self-examination reveals that constructing this connection can make change seem impossible. Approaching Eden, his conscience wakes “the bitter memory / Of what he was, what is, and what must be / Worse” (IV.24-26). The repeated “what” gives past, present, and future equal weight, gathering three different stages of Satan’s existence into one account. The narrator thus presents Satan’s suffering as extending across his whole existence, rather than belonging only to his present condition. Yet he wishes he had been created “some inferior angel,” imagining he would have remained “happy” (59-60), then acknowledges his “free will and pow’r to stand” (66). How Satan explains his fall shapes whether he sees himself as capable of choosing differently or as bound to repeat his past.

Those competing explanations also shape the future Satan allows himself to imagine. In “Which way I fly is Hell; myself am Hell” (IV.75), Satan first describes Hell as something he encounters wherever he goes, then “myself am” makes Hell part of his identity rather than just his surroundings. This shift makes escape seem impossible because he would have to change who he is, not simply where he is. Repentance offers that possibility, but Satan predicts a “worse relapse” (100), imagining that regaining his former position would revive his ambition. Satan reconsolidates his sense of himself by selecting his past rebellion as the pattern for his imagined future, making continued rebellion seem consistent with who he is and repentance seem incapable of changing him.

Whereas Satan imagines repeating his rebellion, Eve imagines losing her relationship with Adam. Even keeping knowledge “without copartner” promises “the more to draw his love” (IX.821-22), showing that Adam already matters within her wish for superiority. Imagining “another Eve,” however, changes their relationship from something she could improve through unequal knowledge into something she could lose altogether. Her resolution that Adam will share “in bliss or woe” (831) reveals that preserving their connection now outweighs ensuring his happiness.

After deciding to share the fruit, Eve presents Adam as her principal reason for seeking godhead, claiming it was something “for thee / Chiefly I sought” (IX.877–78). Placing “for thee” before “I sought” foregrounds Adam before naming her action, while “Chiefly,” at the start of the next line, ranks him as her principal motive without claiming he was her only one. The narrator’s “excuse” and “Apologie” (853-54) frame this account as persuasion rather than transparent recollection. Still, persuasion and partial belief need not exclude each other. Having decided that knowledge without Adam would be intolerable, Eve may now understand her earlier pursuit through that decision. Although the text does not establish her sincerity, the difference between her private deliberation and her account to Adam shows how preserving continuity can conceal changed priorities.

For Satan and Eve, reconsolidation makes their past fit the course they now want to pursue; Adam shows how reconsidering past experience can instead give someone a reason to change course. “Calling to mind with heed / Part of our sentence” (X.1030-31), Adam recalls the promise that Eve’s offspring will bruise the serpent’s head and asks her to remember God’s care during judgment. Whereas Satan expects his past ambition to defeat repentance, Adam reconsiders God’s judgment and care as grounds for attempting it. Adam and Eve return to confess their faults without treating those faults as their entire future.

Milton uses reconsolidation to show how consciousness selects and reinterprets experience to construct continuity between past and future. Satan treats his rebellion as proof that he would rebel again, Eve presents her earlier ambition as chiefly motivated by Adam, and Adam recalls God’s care during judgment to imagine a future beyond condemnation. The freedom at stake lies in being able to rethink who you are, even when you did not choose the ideas that shaped you.

Fruit 04 · Build 4

Build Four

In Build 3, I argued that Milton uses reconsolidation to show how consciousness reinterprets experience to build continuity between past and future. One might object that reconsolidation does not fit Milton’s conception of consciousness, because his characters judge their pasts rather than reinterpret them. Reconsolidation involves reconsidering past experiences in light of present circumstances and possible futures, but conscience, the part of consciousness that judges one’s own actions, seems to leave little room for revision. As Satan approaches Eden, his “conscience wakes despair” (IV.23), and he questions himself: “Hadst thou the same free will and pow’r to stand? / Thou hadst” (66-67). His answer places free will in the past tense, so remembering his freedom accurately only confirms that it is gone. He extends this certainty into the future, predicting that restoration “would but lead me to a worse relapse” (100), allowing only one outcome, and he even claims God shares it: “This knows my punisher” (103). In Book X, Adam’s “reasonings, though through mazes, lead me still / But to my own conviction” (X.830-31). Conviction means both firm belief and guilty verdict, so however his thoughts wander, they end in self-judgment. Eve does reinterpret her past, but the narrator calls her account to Adam an “excuse” (IX.853). Without conscience, nothing would separate rethinking who you are from deceiving yourself. Yet if honest reflection produces judgment while reinterpretation produces excuses, reconsolidation seems to have no place in the poem.

The objection treats Satan’s judgment as the end of his reflection, but Satan keeps reasoning. Explaining why he will not repent, he says, “Disdain forbids me and my dread of shame / Among the spirits beneath whom I seduced” (IV.82-83). “Disdain forbids” gives Satan's pride the authority to prohibit repentance, so the obstacle he presents as unavoidable comes from within his own consciousness. His admission of free will judges the past, but his prediction of relapse treats it as proof of what he must become. Even his appeal to God rests on his own refusal: “as far / From granting He as I from begging peace” (103-4) measures God’s distance by Satan’s, making his own choice the standard. When he concludes, “All hope excluded thus” (105), “thus” points back to his reasoning, showing that his hopelessness is his own conclusion. Satan is reconsolidating after all, using his freedom to interpret his past in a way that denies his freedom to change.

On Eve, the objection is partly correct. When she says godhead was something “for thee / Chiefly I sought” (IX.877-78), placing Adam at the end of one line and ranking him first at the beginning of the next, she rearranges her motives to prioritize him. Yet privately, she imagined becoming his “Superior” (825). Eve does not simply reinterpret her past; she selectively represents it, concealing the ambition she privately entertained. The objection is right that Milton condemns this, which is why the narrator calls it an “excuse.” By hiding her ambition, she avoids confronting the motives she would need to change. But reconsolidation never changes what happened, only what it means for the future, so Eve’s excuse marks its limit rather than ruling it out.

Like Satan, Adam reaches an honest judgment against himself, but Michael shows it need not end in hopelessness when he tells Adam, “Only add / Deeds to thy knowledge answerable, add faith, / Add virtue, patience, temperance, add love” (XII.581-83). “Answerable” makes future deeds accountable to past knowledge without letting that knowledge determine them. Even “Only,” which seems restrictive, introduces an expanding list. Michael’s words come from outside Adam, but every “add” is an imperative only Adam can carry out, so his future is something he must build rather than something his past has already decided.

Milton complicates the original definition of reconsolidation by distinguishing between reconsidering the past and changing what it means for the future. Conscience requires acknowledging what happened, and attempts to evade that responsibility become excuses. Yet Milton’s use of reconsolidation suggests that consciousness can change what the past leads to. Satan treats his past like a physical cause, believing that because he rebelled once, he must rebel again. Michael asks Adam to respond to his past instead, treating his previous actions as something to answer for rather than a pattern he must repeat. For Milton, reconsolidation does not guarantee freedom, but it gives consciousness a way to confront the past without allowing it to dictate the future.

Fruit 05 · Build 5

Build Five

Milton and Montaigne both explore reconsolidation through a self that looks back on what it has become and wishes it were otherwise. Montaigne describes himself as “very ill-formed” and concedes, “But now it is done” (610). Satan voices the same wish when he imagines that, created “some inferior angel,” he would have remained “happy” (IV.59-60). Both writers treat the facts of the past as finished, yet both leave open what those facts mean for the future. Montaigne insists, “I may presently change, not only by chance, but also by intention” (611), and Michael’s command that Adam “add / Deeds to thy knowledge answerable” (XII.581-82) similarly places the future in Adam’s hands. For both, reconsolidation describes consciousness returning to a past it cannot change and deciding what that past will mean.

Montaigne, however, doubts that reconsolidation has a stable self to work on. “What suffers change does not remain one and the same,” he argues (456), so a self that feels different passions at different moments cannot form a single continuous thing. He grants that atoms might reassemble “so that another Montaigne will be born,” yet says this “does not touch me personally, because of oblivion and the interruption of life.” Memory, for Montaigne, forms the only thread connecting one version of a self to another, and once it breaks, continuity breaks with it. “I do not portray being: I portray passing” (611) makes reconsolidation the ordinary condition of self-knowledge, since every portrait holds true only for the moment that produced it.

Milton instead makes continuity a requirement of conscience, though his characters use it in opposite ways. Satan’s memory gathers “what he was, what is, and what must be / Worse” (IV.25-26) into one sequence, with “must be” turning his present despair into a fixed future. Adam’s repentance depends on memory too, “Calling to mind with heed / Part of our sentence” (X.1030-31), but he uses it to find a reason for hope. In other words, Satan treats his past as something that decides his future, while Adam treats it as something he must respond to. Either way, both carry their past forward, so Montaigne’s “oblivion” would offer an escape in Milton’s poem, where a self with no memory of its fall would have nothing to answer for. Eve shows what happens when someone escapes that burden without forgetting. Privately, she imagines becoming Adam’s “Superior” (IX.825), yet she tells him godhead was something “for thee / Chiefly I sought” (877-78). Where Montaigne would record both motives as true of their separate moments, Eve merges them into one consistent story, and the narrator’s labeling it an “excuse” (853) marks that unity as a self-serving revision.

Yet beneath these differences lies a common insight, namely that reconsolidation stays honest only when consciousness recognizes how its present circumstances shape the way it reinterprets the past. Montaigne admits he cannot tell “whether I am different myself, or whether I take hold of my subjects in different circumstances and aspects” (611). He cannot separate a change in himself from a change in how he looks at himself, so every portrait carries the circumstances of its making. Satan and Eve face the same uncertainty, and their failures come from ignoring it. Satan treats his present despair as knowledge of his future, and Eve treats her present decision as the motive she always had. Adam succeeds because recalling only “Part of our sentence” shows he knows he selects from his past to meet a present need. I began, as our definition does, by treating the present as a fixed point from which consciousness views past and future, but reading Montaigne alongside Milton convinced me that consciousness must examine the present as well.

Milton and Montaigne both use reconsolidation in order to show that consciousness can confront a past it cannot change without letting that past dictate its future. Yet, how can reconsolidation allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality and influence its course? In a causal chain, each event simply passes its effects to the next, while reconsolidation lets consciousness return to an earlier event and change what it leads to. Adam and Satan inherit the same fall, yet Adam returns to it and finds hope while Satan sees only a future that “must be / Worse,” so the same past produces opposite results depending on how each reads it. Montaigne adds that consciousness can question its own present state, which keeps that state from acting on him unnoticed. This ability to reread a fixed past frees consciousness from causal determination, since a physical cause can only pass on its effect. Reading Milton alongside Montaigne shows that this freedom lasts only while consciousness keeps questioning its own present, because a mind certain of its present view, like Satan’s, turns its past back into a chain of causes.
