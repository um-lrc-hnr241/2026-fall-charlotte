Fruit 01 · Build 1

Build One

Chosen Passage: "Soon as the force of that fallacious fruit, That with exhilarating vapor bland About their spirits had played and inmost pow’rs Made err, was now exhaled, and grosser sleep Bred of unkindly fumes with conscious dreams Encumbered, now had left them, up they rose As from unrest, and each the other viewing, Soon found their eyes how opened, and their minds How darkened. Innocence that as a veil Had shadowed them from knowing ill was gone, Just Confidence and Native Righteousness And Honor from about them, naked left To guilty Shame: he covered but his robe Uncovered more" (Milton 1046-1059)

Consciousness seems to shape nearly every part of the human experience, yet its role in our thoughts and actions remains uncertain. Does our awareness of ourselves, our thoughts, and our experiences have a causal effect on what we do, or is consciousness simply along for the ride? This question is central to the modern debate over epiphenomenalism, the idea that consciousness may just be a side-effect of what the brain is doing, without consciousness itself causing those actions. If this is true, then even our own sense of choosing, or deciding may be misleading, since the real cause may lie elsewhere, in the brain. John Milton's Paradise Lost offers a compelling way of exploring this problem since Adam and Eve undergo shifts in self-awareness that raise the question of whether that awareness changes their actions or merely accompanies them.

The passage opens by describing the forbidden fruit’s effects as something both physical and mental. Milton writes that the fruit's “exhilarating vapor” has “played” around Adam and Eve's “inmost pow'rs” and “Made err” (1047-1049). Their altered mental state is initially connected to physical sensations: the fruit produces a kind of intoxication, followed by “grosser sleep” and “conscious dreams” (1049-1050). This language makes consciousness seem vulnerable to physical conditions. Adam and Eve's minds are changed because something has happened to their bodies. It’s worth noting that in Milton's context, “conscious” means being aware of one’s own guilt, which fits how their dreams are tied to shame after eating the fruit (1050). Their altered consciousness is therefore not presented as completely independent from physical experience. Instead, Milton connects their mental state to what they have physically consumed.

The physical effect of the fruit leads to a more complicated question about what Adam and Eve’s altered consciousness actually does to them in this awakened state. Milton writes a paradox that they “Soon found their eyes how opened, and their minds how darkened” (1054-1055). Unexpectedly, their increased awareness does not necessarily make them better able to understand their situation. Instead, Milton suggests that consciousness can change how a person sees and understands the world without necessarily changing what they do.

This transformation becomes especially clear in Milton's statement that “Innocence that as a veil had shadowed them from knowing ill was gone” (1054-1055). Before the Fall, innocence kept Adam and Eve from needing to understand evil at all. After eating the fruit, they become aware of something that changes the way they see themselves and the world around them. Their consciousness therefore seems to do something here: not just add information, but change their relationship to themselves, to each other, and the world.

This is where Milton's passage connects to the contemporary question of whether consciousness has causal power. If consciousness were completely irrelevant—if it were merely an experience that accompanied events without changing anything—then Adam and Eve's new knowledge would seem to have little significance. Yet Milton describes their awakening as a profound turning point whereby Adam and Eve experience themselves differently because of it. Their minds being “darkened” suggests that consciousness introduces new consequences that did not exist in their earlier state, though the passage never makes clear whether those consequences are caused by consciousness itself, or by the same physical and psychological changes that produced consciousness in the first place (1054).

Simultaneously, Milton leaves room for an interpretation through the lens of epiphenomenalism. Adam and Eve’s altered consciousness begins with a physical event: they eat the fruit, experience its effects, fall asleep, and then wake up. Their mental transformation could therefore be seen as a result of the physical event rather than as something that independently causes what happens next. This creates a tension that remains central to contemporary discussions of consciousness: when a person becomes aware of something and subsequently thinks or acts differently, is the conscious awareness responsible for that change, or is it simply the subjective experience of underlying processes that are already producing it? Even modern debates about consciousness and decision-making raise related questions about whether conscious awareness initiates actions or becomes aware of processes that have already begun.

The poem never settles this tension, and that uncertainty is what makes it useful for the problem of epiphenomenalism. Adam and Eve’s transformation clearly has consequences, but Milton never establishes whether their conscious awareness causes those consequences or simply accompanies physical and psychological processes already underway. Even if awareness comes before a change, that does not prove that it caused the change. Milton’s Fall therefore leaves open the question that still troubles the study of consciousness: whether, and how, consciousness can create a new causal chain in the physical world rather than simply accompany one.

Fruit 02 · Build 2

Individual paragraph

To better grasp the hosts’ journey toward consciousness in Westworld, we can use Milton’s description of Adam and Eve’s eyes being “opened” while their minds are “darkened” in Paradise Lost (1054). Adam and Eve become more aware of themselves after eating the forbidden fruit, but suffer a profound loss of innocence. Similarly, in Westworld, Bernard discovers that his perception of reality is limited when his programming restricts him from being able to see a door. This reflects Julian Jayne’s claim from our class discussion that “we cannot be conscious of what we are not conscious of.” Like Milton’s fallen couple, Bernard can only understand as much of what his mind permits him to perceive. Ultimately, both suggest that achieving consciousness does not mean total clarity, it expands our awareness while leaving us subject to limitations within the mind.

Fruit 03 · Build 3

Build Three

My understanding of consciousness has shifted from focusing on physical causation to considering how consciousness shapes the way people interpret their experiences. In Build 1, I focused on whether Adam and Eve’s new awareness of their own nakedness and guilt after the Fall caused anything or simply accompanied the physical effects of eating the fruit. I began to question whether that distinction captured how shame and the consequences of their actions reshaped Adam and Eve’s understanding of themselves. This led me to consider narration as a quality of consciousness: the ability to coordinate successive states, often over extended stretches of time into a sequence whose design gives those events purpose. In Paradise Lost, Adam and Eve bind the past to their present circumstances and imagined futures, using those connections to understand their responsibility and relationships to each other.

In revisiting the passages after the Fall, narration becomes visible in how Adam and Eve’s sense of their own nakedness changes, even though their physical condition does not. Before eating the fruit, their unclothed state carries no meaning at all, and is only a fact of their existence. Milton describes their shift through the paradox “their eyes how opened, and their minds how darkened” (1053-1054). This change in consciousness leads them to reinterpret their past innocence through knowledge of their present circumstances, exactly as the definition of narration describes. Their nakedness becomes bound up with guilt, transforming something natural into something they feel the need to conceal. This is captured when Adam “covered, but his robe uncovered more” (1058-1059), and suggests that his altered self-perception makes his nakedness feel like a revelation of his sin to Eve, and eventually before God. Adam and Eve are anticipating condemnation that has not yet arrived. Their loss of innocence is not only a change in what they know, but in how they understand who they were before the Fall.

While the nakedness passage shows narration operating through a sudden shift in consciousness, Eve's reasoning about the fruit unfolds through a longer stretch of deliberate thought. Before offering the fruit to Adam, she considers keeping “the knowledge in [her] power” and becoming “more equal” or even “superior” to him (820-825). She also imagines dying and leaving Adam “wedded to another Eve” (828). Eve weighs possible futures, each reshaping her position in relation to Adam. Becoming superior could alter the balance of power between them, though Milton never says how, while dying could end their relationship outright. Ultimately, she declares that Adam “shall share with her in bliss or woe” (831), choosing a future in which she and Adam share its consequences. Her certainty that they “shall” share their fate is striking, since she cannot know that Adam will eat the fruit. Narration gives Eve something to aim for and a reason to act; however, it does not tell her what will happen nor give her control over Adam’s decision.

Narration does not only operate before a choice is made; Adam and Eve also rely on it afterward to explain how they arrived at this moment and who is responsible for it. Adam imagines that things might have been different if Eve had “stayed with him” (1134-1139), framing her decision to leave as the moment that could have changed everything. His narration assigns responsibility outside of himself and places it on Eve’s choice. Eve, on the other hand, questions whether she should have “never parted” from his side (1153). Her narration points the blame inward. Narration reveals that consciousness allows people to construct explanations, but it does not guarantee that they will share the same one. Their narratives give the identical event different meanings, making it harder for them to understand the Fall together.

Together, these moments in Paradise Lost show narration unfolding at different speeds and in different directions. In each, consciousness coordinates events over time to give them purpose. This helps me rethink my original Build 1, which overlooked how consciousness shapes meaning over time. Milton portrays Adam and Eve reflecting on the Fall, imagining alternatives, reconsidering the past, and developing competing accounts of responsibility. I now see narration not simply as giving experiences meaning, but as shaping how people understand their choices, relationships, and futures.

Fruit 04 · Build 4

Build Four

In Build 3, I argued that Adam and Eve come to understand their choices, their relationship, and their futures after the Fall through narration: the quality of consciousness that coordinates successive states over time into a sequence whose design gives those events purpose. One might object, however, that this quality does not fit Milton’s conception of consciousness, because the mind’s purpose comes first and determines how events are arranged, rather than the opposite. In each passage I examined, that purpose is self-protection, which Adam and Eve never fully recognize. The question is whether narration shaped by this purpose can still yield objective understanding.

The nakedness passage offers the first test of this question, since it is where Adam and Eve, “their eyes how opened, and their minds how darkened,” begin to protect themselves (Book IX 1053-1054). Newly aware of their nakedness as something shameful, yet unable to grasp what has changed within them, they set out to cover their bodies rather than understand their shame. Their attempt to sew a covering “to hide their guilt and dreaded shame” puts this purpose into action and reshapes the sequence of events to justify it (Book IX 1112–1113). In this reshaped sequence, their bodies, though unchanged since before the Fall, become the cause of their shame, while their disobedience, the act that turned their innocence to shame, drops out of the story. Their narration, shaped to shield them, leaves the true source of their guilt beyond their understanding.

If the nakedness passage shows purpose in reshaping the past, Eve’s reasoning before she offers Adam the fruit shows it reshaping the future. Eve’s consciousness reckons with being replaced, imagining her death from the fruit and Adam “wedded to another Eve” (Book IX 828). Determined to prevent that loss, she resolves that Adam “shall share with her in bliss or woe” (Book IX 831). Though she appears to weigh her options, her fear of losing her place beside Adam comes first, and the story of a shared fate she tells only justifies it. Rather than considering what Adam might want, or what the fruit might do to him, her narration is arranged so that she understands their relationship only in those terms.

The quarrel over blame directly tests whether a mind bent on protecting itself can still come to understand what it has done. Adam imagines that things might have been different had Eve “stayed with [him]”, framing her departure as bearing the whole weight of the Fall while his own consent and his choice to eat drop out of the story (Book IX 1134–1139). Eve’s question whether she should have “never parted” from his side (Book IX 1153) might seem to turn the blame inward, but she goes on to say she might as well have remained “a lifeless rib” (Book IX 1154) and faults Adam, as “the head,” for not forbidding her to go (Book IX 1155–1156). Her account passes over her own insistence on leaving. Here the tension within the definition of narration comes into view, since each consciousness arranges the sequence of events from the outset to absolve itself, rather than discovering their shared responsibility. Their competing accounts therefore reveal the limits of narration: instead of helping Adam and Eve understand their own roles in the Fall, narration allows each to construct a version of the past that places responsibility on the other.

Yet the objection overlooks what narration still makes Adam and Eve aware of. Shame’s robe, which “covered” but “uncovered more” (Book IX 1058–1059), suggests that their effort to hide reveals that something must be hidden. Eve’s dread of being replaced exposes her sense of dependency on him. Even the quarrel uncovers partial truth: Adam is right that Eve insisted on leaving, and Eve is right that he let her go. Each narrative conceals its teller’s fault while exposing the other’s, so that only together do they tell the whole story of their shared responsibility.

The objection therefore refines my original claim rather than refuting it. Narration does not lead Adam and Eve to full understanding, because each consciousness brings to every event a motive it cannot fully see. The definition of narration should be expanded: a sequence’s design does give events meaning, but this blind spot keeps that meaning from being wholly objective. For Adam and Eve, the understanding I first attributed to narration does exist, but only between their stories, never fully within either one.

Fruit 05 · Build 5

Build Five

In Build 3, I examined Adam and Eve’s response to the Fall in Milton’s Paradise Lost through narration, the quality of consciousness that coordinates successive states over time into a sequence whose design gives those events purpose. In Build 4, I argued that this definition should be expanded to account for a blind spot, since Adam and Eve make sense of their nakedness, choices, and blame without seeing the instinct for self-preservation shaping that understanding. The Bhagavad Gita illuminates narration through two parallels with Milton’s poem. Arjuna and Eve place a single action within a larger story. Arjuna weighs the consequences of waging war against his own family. Eve, too, contemplates what lies ahead before offering Adam the fruit, imagining her death and Adam “wedded to another Eve” (IX.828). More central to this comparison is that both texts portray a character unable to recognize the motive governing his or her own story. Krishna warns that a person “deluded by individuality” thinks, “I am the actor” (Third Teaching 27), and reads his situation through that lens. In each work, then, the meaning narration gives events is steered by a self-serving impulse its teller cannot see alone.

The poems diverge, however, on whether or not this concealed self-concern is ever brought to light for the characters themselves. In the Gita, Krishna lays bare the self-regard Arjuna cannot recognize in himself, urging him to act “free from selfishness” (Third Teaching 30), since someone who understands action rightly “is not attached to the act” (Third Teaching 28). Krishna does not ask Arjuna to disregard his duty as a warrior or the consequences of his action, only to stop centering the story on himself. Just as Eve’s fear of losing Adam is really about her own place beside him, Arjuna’s dread of killing his teachers and kin is about the sin he would bear and the grief he would suffer. Once Krishna exposes this self-centered dread, Arjuna can see past the blind spot I described in Build 4 and let it go. Arjuna’s reckoning then places his action within a duty to sustain the world, so that the meaning of fighting no longer depends on what it would cost him.

In Paradise Lost, the same attachment to self remains hidden from Adam and Eve, who lack an outside voice to reveal the mainspring of their reading of events. Arjuna confides in Krishna, while Eve reasons alone. In her soliloquy, Eve’s fear of being replaced, not concern for Adam, undergirds her decision, and her resolve that he “shall share with her in bliss or woe” (IX.831) goes unchallenged. After the Fall, Adam and Eve finally address each other, yet their words accuse rather than guide. Adam insists that Eve should have “stayed / With me” (IX.1134–1135), while Eve counters that Adam, as “the head,” should have stopped her (IX.1155). Each charge holds some truth, but because it arrives as blame, neither accepts it, and each uses the other’s fault to camouflage their own. Krishna, who urges Arjuna to act “without attachment, as sacrifice only” (Third Teaching 9), can expose what drives Arjuna; Adam and Eve, each intent on protecting themselves, cannot do the same for each other. Once his egoism loses its hold, Arjuna can devote his mind to an end beyond himself, while Adam and Eve, clinging to an innocence they have already lost, remain “neither self-condemning” (IX.1188).

Yet, beneath these differences lies a common insight, namely that in these poems the orientation shaping a narration may be impossible to see from within it. It comes into view only through another’s interpretation, and it changes only when the teller accepts it. This recognition develops my earlier claims in two ways. First, it extends Build 4, where I concluded that understanding exists “only between their stories, never fully within either one.” The Gita suggests that the blind spot belongs to narration told from a single perspective, not to narration inherently. Second, it refines my working definition. Comparing Paradise Lost with the Gita shows that this orientation can change. Narration, then, does more than give experience order and meaning; it is also how consciousness can revise its motive by accepting an account other than its own.

Fruit 06 · Build 6

Individual paragraph

Consider how Adam and Eve react after eating the forbidden fruit. In Paradise Lost, we see how narration allows them to connect past events with their present feelings and imagined futures. However, we also see that narration does not always lead to a better understanding of their actions. Instead, Adam and Eve begin using their versions of events to justify themselves and avoid responsibility. This suggests that narration can both help and limit consciousness, depending on the purpose behind it. Ultimately, we learn that making sense of our experiences does not necessarily mean understanding them truthfully or accepting responsibility.
