Fruit 01 · Build 1

Build One

Chosen Passage: "Soon as the force of that fallacious fruit, That with exhilarating vapor bland About their spirits had played and inmost pow’rs Made err, was now exhaled, and grosser sleep Bred of unkindly fumes with conscious dreams Encumbered, now had left them, up they rose As from unrest, and each the other viewing, Soon found their eyes how opened, and their minds How darkened. Innocence that as a veil Had shadowed them from knowing ill was gone, Just Confidence and Native Righteousness And Honor from about them, naked left To guilty Shame: he covered but his robe Uncovered more" (Milton 1046-1059)

Consciousness seems to shape nearly every part of the human experience, yet its role in our thoughts and actions remains uncertain. Does our awareness of ourselves, our thoughts, and our experiences have a causal effect on what we do, or is consciousness simply along for the ride? This question is central to the modern debate over epiphenomenalism, the idea that consciousness may just be a side-effect of what the brain is doing, without consciousness itself causing those actions. If this is true, then even our own sense of choosing, or deciding may be misleading, since the real cause may lie elsewhere, in the brain. John Milton's Paradise Lost offers a compelling way of exploring this problem since Adam and Eve undergo shifts in self-awareness that raise the question of whether that awareness changes their actions or merely accompanies them.

The passage opens by describing the forbidden fruit’s effects as something both physical and mental. Milton writes that the fruit's “exhilarating vapor” has “played” around Adam and Eve's “inmost pow'rs” and “Made err” (1047-1049). Their altered mental state is initially connected to physical sensations: the fruit produces a kind of intoxication, followed by “grosser sleep” and “conscious dreams” (1049-1050). This language makes consciousness seem vulnerable to physical conditions. Adam and Eve's minds are changed because something has happened to their bodies. It’s worth noting that in Milton's context, “conscious” means being aware of one’s own guilt, which fits how their dreams are tied to shame after eating the fruit (1050). Their altered consciousness is therefore not presented as completely independent from physical experience. Instead, Milton connects their mental state to what they have physically consumed.

The physical effect of the fruit leads to a more complicated question about what Adam and Eve’s altered consciousness actually does to them in this awakened state. Milton writes a paradox that they “Soon found their eyes how opened, and their minds how darkened” (1054-1055). Unexpectedly, their increased awareness does not necessarily make them better able to understand their situation. Instead, Milton suggests that consciousness can change how a person sees and understands the world without necessarily changing what they do.

This transformation becomes especially clear in Milton's statement that “Innocence that as a veil had shadowed them from knowing ill was gone” (1054-1055). Before the Fall, innocence kept Adam and Eve from needing to understand evil at all. After eating the fruit, they become aware of something that changes the way they see themselves and the world around them. Their consciousness therefore seems to do something here: not just add information, but change their relationship to themselves, to each other, and the world.

This is where Milton's passage connects to the contemporary question of whether consciousness has causal power. If consciousness were completely irrelevant—if it were merely an experience that accompanied events without changing anything—then Adam and Eve's new knowledge would seem to have little significance. Yet Milton describes their awakening as a profound turning point whereby Adam and Eve experience themselves differently because of it. Their minds being “darkened” suggests that consciousness introduces new consequences that did not exist in their earlier state, though the passage never makes clear whether those consequences are caused by consciousness itself, or by the same physical and psychological changes that produced consciousness in the first place (1054).

Simultaneously, Milton leaves room for an interpretation through the lens of epiphenomenalism. Adam and Eve’s altered consciousness begins with a physical event: they eat the fruit, experience its effects, fall asleep, and then wake up. Their mental transformation could therefore be seen as a result of the physical event rather than as something that independently causes what happens next. This creates a tension that remains central to contemporary discussions of consciousness: when a person becomes aware of something and subsequently thinks or acts differently, is the conscious awareness responsible for that change, or is it simply the subjective experience of underlying processes that are already producing it? Even modern debates about consciousness and decision-making raise related questions about whether conscious awareness initiates actions or becomes aware of processes that have already begun.

The poem never settles this tension, and that uncertainty is what makes it useful for the problem of epiphenomenalism. Adam and Eve’s transformation clearly has consequences, but Milton never establishes whether their conscious awareness causes those consequences or simply accompanies physical and psychological processes already underway. Even if awareness comes before a change, that does not prove that it caused the change. Milton’s Fall therefore leaves open the question that still troubles the study of consciousness: whether, and how, consciousness can create a new causal chain in the physical world rather than simply accompany one.

Fruit 02 · Build 2

Individual paragraph

To better grasp the hosts’ journey toward consciousness in Westworld, we can use Milton’s description of Adam and Eve’s eyes being “opened” while their minds are “darkened” in Paradise Lost (1054). Adam and Eve become more aware of themselves after eating the forbidden fruit, but suffer a profound loss of innocence. Similarly, in Westworld, Bernard discovers that his perception of reality is limited when his programming restricts him from being able to see a door. This reflects Julian Jayne’s claim from our class discussion that “we cannot be conscious of what we are not conscious of.” Like Milton’s fallen couple, Bernard can only understand as much of what his mind permits him to perceive. Ultimately, both suggest that achieving consciousness does not mean total clarity, it expands our awareness while leaving us subject to limitations within the mind.
