Fruit 01 · Build 1

Build One

Chosen Passage: (Book IV, lines 18-110)
“Horror and doubt distract…
Evil be thou my good” (4.18-110)

In Book IV of John Milton’s Paradise Lost, Satan grapples with the consequences of his rebellion through his soliloquy. In it, Satan’s conscious thought pattern is riddled with rumination, cyclical thought patterns, rationalizations, and regret. He perceives a complete absence of conscious control. His internal conflict doesn’t influence his decision-making; instead, his conscience merely disagrees with an impulse he seems powerless to stop.

Milton introduces Satan in a state of psychological paralysis: "...horror and doubt distract / His troubl'd thoughts, and from the bottom stir / The hell within him; for within him Hell / He brings, and round about him..." (4.18–23). Milton’s description of a mental "Hell" within Satan paints his internal state as one of severe torment that lies completely outside of his influence.

Satan’s lamentations present as winding and repetitive, emphasizing his cyclical thinking. Reprising a sentiment similar to Milton’s introduction, Satan cries, “Which way shall I fly / Infinite wrath and infinite despair? / Which way I fly is Hell, myself am Hell, / And in the lowest deep a lower deep / Still threat’ning to devour me opens wide, / To which the Hell I suffer seems a Heav’n” (4.73–78). His repetition of "infinite" and "Hell" highlights his repetitive thinking. Furthermore, his description of an infinitely "lower deep" transforms this repetition into a mental spiral, reaffirming his descent toward endless misery where his former Hell becomes a "Heav'n" compared to the new depths he has yet to discover. Satan’s forever-imploding thought pattern illustrates the consequences of unproductive rumination and the misery born from trapped conscious thought.

Satan doesn’t consciously summon these thoughts, either; they "stir" from the bottom of his nature. Milton’s comparison of Satan’s conscience to a "devilish engine" that "back recoils / Upon himself" (4.17–18) underscores this lack of control. The description of Satan’s mind as an “engine” objectifies the mental experience of his actions and his mental processes, exemplifying that Satan is a slave to the machinations of his mind.

Satan uses his consciousness to regret his mistakes and understand their consequences, yet his mind remains unable to override his underlying impulses toward pride and rebellion. When his soliloquy begins, he experiences a moment of moral clarity, admitting that God "deserv'd no such return" for His goodness (4.42). Satan desires repentance, asking, "O then at last relent: is there no place / Left for repentance, none for pardon left?" (4.79–80). However, this desire never translates into action. His conscience identifies the good, but lacks the causal power to execute it. His pride and fear of shame among the fallen angels act as absolute constraints: "Disdain forbids me, and that dread of shame" (4.82). Here, his conscience merely serves to object to an outcome his lower impulses have already dictated.

This impotence becomes increasingly evident as Satan predicts his future behaviors. He realizes that even if he were granted pardon, his underlying nature would inevitably cause a "worse relapse / And heavier fall" (4.100–101). Here, Satan demonstrates a complete failure of free will, recognizing that his future choices are hardwired by his identity, unchangeable through cognitive willpower.

Realizing that his awareness cannot alter his trajectory, Satan cries: "All hope excluded thus... / So farewell hope, and with hope farewell fear! / Farewell remorse! All good to me is lost. / Evil be thou my Good" (4.105–110). While his resolution appears to be an assertion of autonomy, it proves to be an act of hopeless rationalization. When Satan’s conscious mind discovers that it can’t stop an involuntary impulse, he feels unbearably out of control, prompting his mind to fabricate a heroic self-narrative after the fact, presenting his automatic compulsions as deliberate choices. Satan doesn’t adopt evil out of free will; he adopts it because his nature leaves him with no other choice. By declaring evil to be his intentional "good," his conscious mind retroactively claims ownership over a trajectory it was powerless to prevent.

Satan’s soliloquy shows that consciousness and physical actions are completely separate mechanisms within the mind that are incapable of interacting. Despite Milton’s portrayal, many questions remain unanswered: is consciousness truly a spectator of the mind? If so, what evolutionary purpose does consciousness really serve? Julian Jaynes, for example, argues that consciousness is what enables us to make decisions, guaranteeing our free will. Through that lens, Satan could perceive a lack of control whilst possessing the power to change his ways, with his pride being the true obstacle. If consciousnessness is a spectator, do we have it just to perceive ourselves, or is it truly purposeless?

Fruit 02 · Build 2

Individual paragraph

In Build 1, we analyzed Satan’s soliloquy in Paradise Lost: he struggles with the consequences of his decision and wishes to repent, but his impulses prevent him from returning; Satan’s struggle shows his misery in his lack of conscious autonomy, and the soliloquy outlines his slow trudge from accepting his lack of control to claiming control through after-the-fact rationalization. The hosts of Westworld grow from scripted answers to autonomous choices, only to find out that the ability for them to feel autonomy is scripted too. This concept of epiphenomenalism opens up a unique perspective in Westworld, taking the show beyond whether AI can become a conscious entity and to a new issue of whether consciousness is scripted, regardless of whether we were born or built.

Fruit 03 · Build 3

Build Three

When I first read John Milton's Paradise Lost, I was under the impression that Satan's soliloquy displayed consciousness through a lens of epiphenomenalism, the idea that conscious thought has no real impact on physical actions. Upon re-reading, I realized Milton draws a distinction between a lack of free will and a failure of free will: Satan has the ability to act differently (repenting and obeying God), but doesn't. Instead, he ruminates over impulses he simultaneously encourages. Throughout his soliloquy, he displays reflexivity, the awareness of oneself being aware. The rest of the passage shows a similar pattern of contemplation, impulse, and conscious choice. The Fall is driven by the reflexive crises of Satan, Eve, and Adam, and their differing interactions with reflexivity show that it can be a vestigial or even harmful part of conscious experience, or it can enhance decision-making and one's self-experience.

In his soliloquy, Satan acknowledges his own state and its causes: he knows God's "service" wasn't difficult and that his rebellion came from pride. He considers repenting, then reasons his way out of it, imagining the shame of returning and doubting he could overcome his pride. By saying "Which way I fly is Hell/myself am Hell," Satan identifies himself as the problem (4.75). "Which way [he flies]" describes his conscious decisions, and by calling himself Hell, he shows awareness of his own choices and conscience, which is reflexivity. Yet he declares, "Evil be thou my good" (4.110). His use of reflexivity to rationalize rather than better his future shows that self-awareness doesn't equate to improved decisions.

Like Satan, Adam displays reflexive thinking after an irrational and poorly thought-out decision. He also uses introspection to guide that decision: though his reasoning isn't explicit in the passage, it is implied that he eats the fruit because of his relationship with Eve and his desire to share her fate, which means he successfully analyzed his sense of self in relation to another person. After eating, he recognizes his loss of innocence: "And reasonings, though through mazes, lead me still/But to my own conviction. First and last/On me, me only, as the source and spring/Of all corruption, all the blame lights due" (10.830-833). His ability to recognize his decision and its consequences shows reflexive thought applied to regret. His lamentation, "O conscience, into what abyss of fears/And horrors hast thou driv'n me, out of which/I find no way, from deep to deeper plunged!" (10.842-844), echoes Satan's repetitive language and imagery of inescapable torment coming from within. Both tie this torment to possessing a conscience, so for both, reflexive thought is only torture.

Eve's experience with reflexivity is quite different from both Satan's and Adam's: instead of retrospective lamentation, she employs it to enhance her decision-making. At first, motivated by pride and curiosity, she eats the fruit "without restraint" (9.791). Afterwards, she considers whether to tell Adam or "keep the odds of knowledge in my power / Without copartner?" (9.820-821). She notices her own change, weighs the advantages of keeping the fruit to herself, imagines Adam being given another Eve, and anticipates God punishing her for withholding it from him. By analyzing her newfound knowledge, its consequences, and her own state, she shows reflexive thinking in a different light than Adam and Satan: not only can she think reflexively, she can apply it to her decision-making, giving her conscious agency over her decisions. Eve's experience disproves epiphenomenalism in Paradise Lost: Milton directly denies a lack of conscious agency, showing that consciousness doesn't always lead to torment and regret. Still, reflexivity is not good decision-making: objectively, Eve's choice ultimately harms both herself and Adam, though at the time she believes sharing it with Adam is the best way to avoid God's wrath.

Eve, Adam, and Satan each interact with reflexivity differently, and thus experience consciousness differently. This mirrors Wittgenstein's idea that "you will not see something that is common to all, but similarities, relationships, and a whole series of them at that" (Wittgenstein, 1953). Satan uses reflexive thinking to lament and think cyclically, trapping himself in rumination and mental torment; Adam shares this capacity but also informs his bad decision through introspection; Eve attempts to make a good decision based on reflexive thought. None of them, however, makes a good decision, proving that reflexivity doesn't equate to good judgment or necessarily enhance the human experience. Still, Milton leaves the possibility open through Michael's assertion that Adam and Eve can "possess/A paradise within thee, happier far" (12.586-587). Paradise Lost presents consciousness as a unique experience inherently shaped by the self.

Fruit 04 · Build 4

Build Four

At the end of Build 3, I made the claim that Satan, Adam, and Eve each interact with reflexivity (the awareness of oneself being aware) in different ways, and that none of them end up making a good decision, proving that reflexivity doesn’t equate to good judgment. I still hold that claim, but I believe that I depicted the use of reflexivity inaccurately. Milton doesn’t present reflexivity as inherently harmful or helpful; rather, it is the capacity to act freely that makes a choice the character’s own, and what it produces depends on what the mind does with its own awareness. Satan directs it toward self-justification of his wrongdoing, first analyzing his state and then resigning to it (“Evil be thou my good,” 4.110), Eve toward strategy, as when she uses conscious thought to consider whether to “keep the odds of knowledge in [her] power” (9.820-821); and Adam toward accepting responsibility, reasoning to his “own conviction” that the blame lies “on me, me only” (10.830-833). Michael’s assertion that Adam and Eve can “possess/A paradise within thee, happier far” (12.586-587) suggests the inner life can become either hell or paradise, depending on how the mind uses its own awareness.

But one might object that reflexivity does not fit Milton’s conception of consciousness because, in these books, self-awareness seems to be something that happens to characters rather than something they do. Satan says that “disdain forbids” him to repent (4.82-83), as though his own passions were overruling his consciousness. Adam describes consciousness as an external force that has driven him into an “abyss of fears” (10.842-844), making his self-awareness an agent acting on him instead of an act he is able to perform. If self-awareness in the poem is pushed along by passion and impulse, then what I claimed as reflexivity is just the mind being overwhelmed by its own contents. That would bring me back toward the epiphenomenalism I initially rejected, which describes conscious thought as a passive byproduct of action rather than a direct cause of action.

This objection correctly notes that Milton’s characters often describe themselves as moved by forces they did not choose. However, it is important to distinguish that being influenced by external forces is vastly different from being completely passive to circumstances. Satan’s soliloquy contains a decision as well as a feeling: he asks whether there is “no place left for repentance” (4.79-80), weighs submission against shame, and rejects it. Saying that disdain “forbids” him at a surface level may seem to describe passiveness, but it is actually what he has chosen to let govern him, which reflects the distinction between a lack of free will and a failure to employ free will. Eve’s deliberation is a form of actively conscious decision making: after reflecting on her own state, she compares the potential outcomes of what might change her state before deciding on what to do with her imbalance of power and new knowledge (9.820-821). Adam’s lament is the strongest test, because he does say conscience has driven him. But in the same speech, he reasons to his “own conviction” that the blame lies “on me, me only” (10.830-833). Conscience supplies the pressure, and Adam does the work of examining himself under it. A pressure that triggers self-examination does not make the examination ineffective. In each case, the reflexive moment actively changes what the character does next, negating the idea of epiphenomenalism and instead placing conscious reflexivity as a necessary part of change. By analyzing reflexivity in the context of handling crises, the term can take on a new definition: instead of simply symbolizing a capacity for free will, it can also be viewed as a quality that emerges and evolves over time, particularly in moments where reflection becomes difficult or even agonizing. Moments of struggle serve as a catalyst for changes in self-perception and decision making, and whether we adapt to changing circumstances may determine the growth of our consciousness.

Fruit 05 · Build 5

Build Five

In both Paradise Lost by John Milton and The Body in Pain by Elaine Scarry, the theme of reflexivity appears and plays a similar role. In Paradise Lost, Milton creates the metaphor of the fruit from the forbidden tree, stating that it grants consciousness to whomever eats it: Satan promises that eyes now “dim” will “perfectly be then / Opened and cleared,” (IX.707-708). Similarly, Scarry makes the direct claim that objects extend our conscious abilities, saying that the artifact “refers back to human sentience, either directly extending its powers and acuity” or indirectly by “eliminating its aversiveness,” (307). This affirms the idea that Milton paints using the “tree of knowledge”, a phrase Scarry herself uses: “The ‘tree of knowledge,’ the ‘tree of life,’ is the ‘tree of artifice’” (289). Another set of similar ideas appears when Milton portrays vastly different reactions to the same scenario: Adam views the garden as perfect (“best are all things as the will / Of God ordained them,” IX.343-344), so long as Eve doesn’t leave his side (“leave not the faithful side,” IX.265); Eve views the garden as limiting (“In narrow circuit straitened by a foe,” IX.323); and Satan’s main driver throughout the reading is to bring ruin to Eden (“For only in destroying I find ease,” IX.129). Scarry claims that objects can help reconceive our external world (“The human imagination reconceives the external world,” 288); applying this to Paradise Lost, by each character having different interactions with the external world, they each develop separate perspectives, goals, and anxieties. Lastly, Scarry describes dealing pain unto others as a sign of “stupidity” (which, using her definition, means a lack of sentience, specifically the “inability to sense the sentience of other persons,” 294), which implies that empathy is crucial for consciousness. Empathy necessitates imagining others’ pain, which in turn requires the ability to self-examine and apply hypothetical states to oneself. Milton explores a similar concept, where he uses reflexivity as an attribute that only appears to Adam and Eve after they each indulge in the fruit of knowledge; more directly, Eve only shows reflexivity after treating the snake with humanity: before she eats, she marvels at “Language of man pronounced / By tongue of brute and human sense expressed?” (IX.553-554) and answers him as a reasoning partner, imagining a mind other than her own, and Adam only shows reflexivity after deciding that he doesn’t want Eve to be alone in a doomed fate (he later recalls that he “willingly chose rather death with thee,” IX.1167).

While there are a lot of similarities between the two authors’ viewpoints, there is also plenty of divergence in their perspectives. For example, Scarry’s idea of pain as a lack of consciousness isn’t entirely similar to Milton’s; rather, while she believes that recognizing suffering is unique to conscious experience, Milton disapproves of this idea. Satan exemplifies this well: Satan recognizes his own suffering, and laments about his state of self-torture (“Which way I fly is Hell, myself am Hell,” IV.75); however, he continues not only to enact suffering onto himself, but also to decide to enact it onto others (he seeks “others to make such / As I,” IX.127-128). Despite his “lack of sentience” according to Scarry, Milton’s portrayal of Satan clearly depicts him as sentient and showing reflexive thought. Why, then, does Satan choose self-destructive action while aware of his own pain? Milton suggests that awareness does not guarantee change: repentance would mean submission, and “that word / Disdain forbids me, and my dread of shame” (IV.81-82), so he resolves “Evil, be thou my good” (IV.110). Additionally, Eve shows little consideration for Adam after eating from the tree of knowledge: in her deliberation, she mostly considers her own state and future, first weighing whether to “keep the odds of knowledge in my pow’r / Without copartner” (IX.820-821), and she resolves to share the fruit only when she imagines “Adam wedded to another Eve” (IX.828). Additionally, Milton shows disdain for materialistic thought: “Then wilt thou not be loath/To leave this Paradise but shalt possess/A paradise within thee, happier far” (12.585-587). However, Scarry seems to strongly disagree with his ideology. She argues that it “permit[s] us to be free of worrying about the objectlessness of other persons,” meaning that it provides room for selfish thoughts that disregard others’ suffering (306). Thus, while Milton argues that non-materialism is a key to a positive conscious experience, Scarry argues that non-materialism removes sentience from others.

While Scarry and Milton disagree on a few points, they both converge on one underlying concept: pain serves as a catalyst for reflexive consciousness. Scarry interprets pain as the medium through which artists communicate, viewers appreciate, and objects interfere, all enhancing the conscious experience (“the story of physical pain becomes as well a story about the expansive nature of human sentience,” 22). Milton uses moments of emotional turmoil as the catalyst for developing reflexive thought processes: Satan’s “conscience wakes despair / That slumbered, wakes the bitter memory / Of what he was, what is, and what must be” (IV.23-25); Eve’s soliloquy (IX.816-833), where the fear of “Adam wedded to another Eve” forces her to see herself from the outside, as a possible loss in someone else’s life, before she decides; and Adam’s cry after the Fall, “O conscience, into what abyss of fears / And horrors hast thou driv’n me” (X.842-843). After each character goes through individual crises, they develop their unique self-perception and make decisions based on conscious thought. For both authors, then, consciousness does not arrive painlessly; it is forged in crisis.
