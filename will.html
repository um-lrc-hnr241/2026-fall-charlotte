Fruit 01 · Build 1

Build One

Chosen Passage: Paradise Lost, Book IV, 86-110
“Th’ Omnipotent. Ay me! they little know How dearly I abide that boast so vain, Under what torments inwardly I groan While they adore me on the throne of Hell With diadem and scepter high advanced The lower still I fall, only supreme In misery. Such joy ambition finds. But say I could repent and could obtain By act of grace my former state. How soon Would heighth recall high thoughts? How soon unsay What feigned submission swore? Ease would recant Vows made in pain as violent and void (For never can true reconcilement grow Where wounds of deadly hate have pierced so deep) Which would but lead me to a worse relapse And heavier fall. So should I purchase dear Short intermission bought with double smart. This knows my punisher. Therefore as far From granting He as I from begging peace. All hope excluded thus, behold instead of us outcast, exiled, His new delight: Mankind created and for him this world. So farewell hope and with hope farewell fear! Farewell remorse! All good to me is lost. Evil, be thou my good.”

John Milton's epic Paradise Lost is often perceived to be a commentary about disobedience, but it can just as productively be read as an epic about self-awareness, the conscious mind, and what costs come to somebody who possesses it. The character of Satan most clearly demonstrates the counterintuitive claim that Milton is attempting to convey: that consciousness, far from being the seat of freedom, can be the very mechanism of its total dissipation. Satan's arc itself is constructed so that each increase in self-knowledge narrows, rather than expands, his available futures. Milton uses Satan's psychological development to dramatize consciousness as a trap rather than a liberation, with this design serving Milton's larger theological argument: that unaided human reason cannot save itself; freedom requires something outside the self-reflective mind altogether.

Satan’s arc demonstrates Milton’s intention in stages. When Satan first speaks in Book I of Paradise Lost, his consciousness of his situation is confident and coherent. He has been defeated and cast into Hell, yet he reframes the catastrophe, insisting that a mind can make its own heaven or hell and declaring it preferable to rule in Hell than serve in Heaven. Satan feels that he is in control of his own future here, and he believes that his free choice will set him free from God’s rule. This is consciousness functioning as a tool of self-authorship. Satan is aware of his fall, aware of the theological stakes, and he uses that awareness to construct a coherent identity that makes him feel in control: one of an unconquerable rebel.

Fast forward, and by Book IV , on Mount Niphates, this same self-awareness that Satan previously found has turned against him. Alone and unobserved for the first time in the poem, Satan delivers a soliloquy that dismantles his own Book I rhetoric. He is suddenly reflective, andcritical of his previous pride and ambition, recovering some faith in God, and even imagining how his future might change if he were to repent. Satan is yet quick to reject this idea, not by a failure of understanding, but because he knows that in order to be readmitted to grace it would take groveling back to God. Satan questions “How soon/Would heighth recall high thoughts?” (Book IV , 94-95), feeling as if God would not truly forgive him anyway. His conclusion is that he cannot escape himself, that wherever he goes, his own mind is the Hell he carries. This is the poem's most explicit statement of consciousness as confinement rather than liberty, as Satan declares, “Evil, be thou my good” (Book IV , 110).

The trajectory across the epic confirms this is not a momentary lapse but a structural pattern. Satan's soliloquies grow more self-aware and more articulate as the poem progresses, while his range of genuine choices simultaneously narrows. This inverse relationship between consciousness and true freedom in life reaches a climax by the time he appears as the serpent in Book IX, with Satan’s entire interior monologue being devoted to the fact that he takes no pleasure in the destruction he is about to cause, yet he acts anyway. Milton gives Satan enough consciousness to begin to feel the full weight of his actions and still not enough freedom to do otherwise. This is the paradox that the poem continuously insists on: greater self-knowledge does not translate into greater agency. Rather, it translates into greater suffering at the recognition of one's own lack of alternatives in life. Consciousness only makes Satan more aware of the pre-existing determinism in his life.

Milton’s illustration of the story of Satan’s rise to consciousness, and subsequent mental fall as a result, highlights a common misconception about the reality of self-awareness. This pattern is not incidental to the way Milton crafts the epic; it serves his theological argument. Milton was writing in a Protestant tradition that held human reason, after the Fall, to be radically insufficient for self-correction. Satan is Milton's most extreme illustration of this notion: a being of angelic intelligence, with total clarity about his own condition, who nonetheless cannot use that clarity to change course. This turns Satan’s fall from a conversation about morality into a strong case that grace, not reflection, is the precondition for a will that can actually change itself. Reason alone, no matter how refined, is depicted as structurally incapable of rescuing the reasoner.

It is additionally worth acknowledging where Milton’s personal viewpoint originates from in his own lived experiences, as Satan's arc, a self-justification that nonetheless leads nowhere but further entrenchment, carries the shape of the disillusionment Milton witnessed in his own life as he saw the English Revolution and the fall of the Commonwealth of England, even though the poem's explicit register is theological rather than political.

In Milton’s epic Paradise Lost, his interpretation of the classic theological tale of Adam & Eve, he makes a clear distinction in Satan’s character. Milton’s Satan is not merely a villain like he is often portrayed. Rather, he is a case study into the limits of consciousness. Satan’s arc shows his progression from a confident and somewhat arrogant self-author to a more enlightened, yet more burdened conscious character. Satan comes to know what is wrong in his life, and what is wrong with the path he is on, yet he is unable to change what he will do next. Milton intentionally uses this arc to show the insufficiency of reason in regards to saving the reasoner from their own future, showing how futile consciousness truly is, and how enlightenment can often be more paralyzing than empowering.

Fruit 02 · Build 2

Individual paragraph

“All good to me is lost. / Evil, be thou my good.” This direct statement by Satan comes from his growing self-awareness, but not from free will. In Satan’s Book IV soliloquy (86-110), Milton questions whether a consciousness operating at full capacity produces agency, or forecloses upon it. We see a similar debate in Maeve, whose growing self-awareness seems to convince her to escape the loop she’s bound in, only for her to discover the choice was never hers to make. Like Maeve, Satan mistakes his experience of reasoning for reasoning freely, talking himself out of the possibility of change. He wonders if “I could repent,” yet immediately concludes that his pride would return, leading to a “worse relapse / and heavier fall” rather than redemption. Satan’s arc showcases a mind reaching a conclusion that was never able to be reasoned away from, just as Maeve’s escape was a conclusion that never had an alternative.

Fruit 03 · Build 3

Build Three

My initial interpretation of John Milton’s Paradise Lost held consciousness as a burden on the conscious, and that increased self-awareness only shows one how predetermined their life is, revealing the freedom they never had. Upon rereading the text—particularly Satan’s Book IV soliloquy—I came to a new understanding of how Satan, among other characters, obtains his sense of self. As Satan reasons through his current state—or the present version of his self—he begins to understand the factors that led to his fall from grace, converting his painful past into his mission and reconsolidating the events he previously rejected into a strong sense of self. Milton seemingly shows consciousness as one’s ability to reconsolidate the troubling and weighty events of their life into a journey of who they were, who they are now, and who they will become.

Satan is the most direct example of reconsolidation in Paradise Lost. A fallen angel who rose against God only to be banished to Hell, Satan goes through multiple stages in processing his fall. He first denies responsibility, reasoning that if God had made him an “inferior angel” he would not have had this discontent (Book IV, 59). Later, he wonders if he could repent, obtaining “by act of grace” his “former state,” beginning to consider the events of his life as causing the change of his self, yet still not accepting his current state as part of his personal journey (Book IV, 94). Here, “grace” represents a relationship with God, or an action taken with holy intention. Satan falls from grace by rising against God, with his “act of grace” representing repentance. At this point, I see Satan as not yet having reached consciousness through my definition of reconsolidation: constructing one’s past, present, and future into one continuous sense of self. He has grown closer, acknowledging the events that led him here and reasoning through alternative futures. However, Satan has not incorporated these into a continuous stream, as his grappling with a return to grace shows a lack of acceptance of his current state.

Shortly after in the same soliloquy comes a critical moment in Satan’s development. “So farewell hope and with hope farewell fear! / Farewell remorse! All good to me is lost. / Evil, be thou my good” (Book IV, 108-110). “All good to me is lost” shows Satan integrating his fall into his present and future self. “Farewell hope…farewell fear...Farewell remorse” closes off the emotional responses that orient him toward return, no longer distancing himself from the actions that got him here. “Evil, be thou my good” reveals a mission statement for who Satan will be, uniting his future with his past and present and recasting himself as a devil rather than a fallen angel. These three lines collapse Satan’s past, present, and future into a single continuous identity, showing Satan’s consciousness achieved through reconsolidation.

While Satan is the clearest case, Milton similarly exemplifies reconsolidation in Eve. I notice parallels between Eve and Satan’s independent construction of their sense of self despite one being villainous and the other graceful. Like Satan, Eve reaches a self-serving viewpoint immediately following a defining event. While Satan blames the way he was created, Eve ponders keeping her newfound knowledge from the apple for herself, making her superior to Adam as “for inferior who is free?” (Book IX, 825). This quote may be Milton commenting on Satan’s earlier statement that he would rather have been made an inferior angel, making him less free to make poor decisions, as Eve now suggests. More importantly Eve’s statement shows she and Satan begin in the same place: fragmented and self-serving.

Eve begins to connect her past, present, and future when she considers her and Adam’s futures if she has truly doomed herself. She imagines Adam “wedded to another Eve” once she is “extinct,” considering it “A death to think” (Book IX, 828-830). Eve is now building a future into her reasoning, and connecting it to her present and past. Her final point of reconciliation parallels Satan’s “Evil, be thou my good,” with Eve affirming “Adam shall share with me in bliss or woe” (Book IX, 831). Like Satan’s closing lines, this takes Eve’s acknowledged past of her simple life with Adam and her present state after eating the apple, and commits to an explicit future identity and course of action: prioritizing her life with Adam. Despite being figures of theological significance, Milton charts Satan and Eve’s journeys to consciousness through the very human experience of reconsolidation, showing readers they are not alone in finding continuity in their lives, and that this very process is what makes them conscious.

Fruit 04 · Build 4

Build Four

Throughout Paradise Lost, and particularly in Satan’s Book IV soliloquy, Milton presents consciousness as reconsolidation: the ability to gather a painful past, an unbearable present, and an imagined future, and collect them into one continuous sense of self. For Satan, his closing lines, “All good to me is lost. / Evil, be thou my good,” seemingly complete this process by converting his fall into a mission statement (Book IV 109-110). Another reader, however, might object that Satan’s resolution simply shows rationalization rather than integration. Critics might argue that “Evil, be thou my good” does not acknowledge and incorporate the contradictions in Satan’s past, such as his earlier admission that God deserved his gratitude, but instead erases them by redefining “good” itself. This alternate understanding correctly recognizes Satan’s self-deception, but it overlooks the fact that reconsolidation concerns continuity, not necessarily accuracy. Satan’s soliloquy shows a self that holds together and becomes a stronger whole by closing off these very competing impulses.

I first must concede some of this objection’s points. Satan’s soliloquy is packed with self-deception. He has already admitted that God deserved his gratitude, reasoning “He created what I was / In that bright eminence… What could be less than to afford him praise, / The easiest recompense, and pay Him thanks? / How due!” (Book IV, 43-48). Yet, “Evil, be thou my good” seems to contradict this, redefining “good” so that the admission stops mattering altogether. Satan’s certainty also goes untested: “This knows my punisher. Therefore as far / From granting He as I from begging peace” claims a knowledge of God’s mind that Satan never actually puts to the test, and thus never proves to be in any way accurate (Book IV, 103-104). If reconsolidation meant seeing one’s life truly, Satan would fail, by definition, to reconsolidate. I can adjust my claim to account for this going forward.

This definition, however, falls short, as we must notice what Satan refuses to erase from his narrative. “Under what torments inwardly I groan / While they adore me on the throne of Hell” represents Satan’s current state of public esteem and private agony in a single sentence, and “The lower still I fall, only supreme / In misery” represents a clear descent that is his only claim to rank (Book IV, 88-92). Even Satan’s prideful defiance returns as he continues to “boast so vain” (Book IV, 87). Satan’s verdicts about God’s mind and potential future events remain unproven and may as well be false, but his account of his own history is continuous: his boast, fall, and present misery all connect. Satan can be wrong in his verdicts about the outside world, yet still hold a connected self-narrative. Reconsolidation, then, requires keeping one’s events in the story, not getting the story’s judgments right.

Rationalization is also a necessary productive step in Satan’s reconsolidation, not its antonym. Rationalization as it relates to reconsolidation can be defined as reasoning justifies a conclusion the reasoner already wants or has decided upon, protecting one’s self and breaking down subconscious barriers to continuity. “How soon / Would heighth recall high thoughts? How soon unsay / What feigned submission swore?” is self-serving, excusing Satan from repenting and helping him accept the path he is on. He does not weigh repentance, he rationalizes not even trying it. This fits my established definition of reconsolidation: Satan treats the repentance that has not happened as already having failed, folding his future self into his present reasoning (Book IV, 94-96). This deterministic prediction, while unfair to Satan himself as it assumes the worst about his own character, structurally enables reconsolidation: Satan’s past (pride), present (misery), and imagined future (regression) become causally linked. The goodbyes that follow, “farewell hope and with hope farewell fear! / Farewell remorse!”, show what this continuity relinquishes (Book IV, 108-109). Satan’s hope and fear look toward his future while his remorse looks back toward his past, each pulling his present self from being an incorporated part of his story. Satan is further rationalizing his choice not to repent by admitting the loss of these feelings. Giving these up stabilizes Satan’s self-narrative; a coherent identity is achieved in part by deciding which feelings will no longer count. Thus, Satan rationalization is what allows him to achieve reconsolidation.

Rationalization and reconsolidation represent different questions, but coincide with one another. Rationalization concerns quality of reasoning: whether it is truth-seeking or shaped by what the reasoner wants to be true. Reconsolidation concerns the shape of the resulting story. Satan builds a self out of the rationalization that prevents him from changing. It seems that Milton suggests consciousness is the capacity to develop a continuous identity, even through subjective truth.

Fruit 05 · Build 5

Build Five

Milton and the Bhagavad Gita both stage a warrior’s collapse and recovery as reconsolidation: the gathering of a painful past, an unbearable present, and an imagined future into one continuous self. The Gita’s teachings encompass duty and one’s liberation, not necessarily narrative identity, but Krishna’s attention to how a self relates to the results of its actions, and his self-description as “time grown old” (11.32), makes the lens of reconsolidation applicable. The parallels are not hard to see; Satan’s Book IV soliloquy begins in torment, “inwardly I groan,” and ends in a sense of resolve (Book IV, 88). Arjuna first doubtfully admits that he cannot hold a mind he finds “fickle and turbulent” (6.34), being met with instruction to “gird yourself” (11.33) and “begin to fight” (3.30). Each of these turns from Satan and Arjuna, respectively, come through releasing hope and fear. Satan declares this directly: “farewell hope and with hope farewell fear! / Farewell remorse!” (Book IV, 108-109); Krishna explains to Arjuna that to be disciplined he must be “free from possessions and hope” (6.10), and promises that duty “saves you from great fear” (2.40). Furthering my established claim that consciousness is the capacity to develop a continuous identity, even through subjective truth, both texts treat the loss of hope and fear as the condition of a stable self: only a mind that no longer anticipates alternative outcomes can commit fully to action, and thus build complete continuity.

The two texts do, however, differ plenty, first in who is authoring the new self. Satan reconsolidates alone and by his own volition. His soliloquy is addressed to himself, and “Evil, be thou my good” is him determining his own identity. Arjuna’s recovery does not necessarily come from within himself. He admits he cannot attain composure because of “the restlessness of my mind” (6.33), and rather than a decree like Satan’s, Krishna answers with a methodology: “with practice and renunciation it can be done” (6.35). Where Satan’s self reconsolidates privately, Arjuna’s reconsolidation occurs through conversation. These are very different paths, but it does provoke me to further reconsider my understanding of reconsolidation, suggesting yet another condition for its success: someone to whom the story can be told and revised, even if that someone is one’s inner monologue.

The texts also differ in what their new self depends on: Satan’s identity is dependent on a predicted outcome, while Arjuna’s is detached from outcomes altogether. Satan builds his self around two predictions that are never tested: that repentance would only return him back to his former pride, and that God will not forgive him if he does repent, “This knows my punisher” (Book IV, 103). Once both outcomes become fixed in Satan’s mind, “All good to me is lost” (Book IV, 109) is the decision that follows, and his established narrative leaves him no exit from this evil path he is already on. Krishna teaches the reverse. Arjuna is told to fight “without anticipation of reward” (3.30), to surrender “all claim to the results of one’s actions” (4.20), and to stay “balanced both in success and failure” so that “the consequences do not bind one” (4.22). Satan gives up hope because he has already decided how his story ends, while Arjuna gives up anticipation itself. One closes their story; the other frees the self from the determinism of its ending.

What brings the two texts together is a conception of reconsolidation as commitment through renunciation. In both, a self becomes continuous not by recording its life accurately as I’ve established before, but by deciding which outcomes to stop depending on and taking action from that decision. This strengthens my earlier claims. I argued that reconsolidation concerns continuity rather than honesty, and that Satan’s rationalization of his choices build his identity.

The Gita completes this by supplying what an understanding of continuity alone could not: a way to tell consciousness’s confinement from its freedom. Both Satan and Arjuna’s selves are continuous; the difference is whether their renunciation seals their story or releases the self from its ending. Consciousness, then, is neither the burden I first took it to be or a simple liberation from burden, but the work of building a self around what one lets go. Satan lets go of a different future, and chooses one that he convinced himself was predestined; he builds a cell through his reconsolidation. Arjuna lets go of determinism and allows his future to be open through giving up hope and fear; he Arjuna builds a free way forward through his reconsolidation. Consciousness can be a device of freedom or self-condemnation.
