Fruit 01 · Build 1

Build One

Passage: “To him she hasted. In her face excuse Came prologue and apology too prompt Which with bland words at will she thus addressed: Hast thou not wondered, Adam, at my stay? Thee I have missed and thought it long, deprived Thy presence, agony of love till now Not felt, nor shall be twice, for never more Mean I to try what rash untried I sought: The pain of absence from thy sight. But strange Hath been the cause and wonderful to hear! This tree is not as we are told a tree Of danger tasted nor to evil unknown Op’ning the way but of divine effect To open eyes and make them gods who taste, And hath been tasted such! The serpent wise, Or not restrained as we or not obeying, Hath eaten of the fruit and is become Not dead, as we are threatened, but thenceforth Endued with human voice and human sense Reasoning to admiration and with me Persuasively hath so prevailed that I Have also tasted and have also found Th’ effects to correspond: opener mine eyes, Dim erst, dilated spirits, ampler heart And growing up to godhead which for thee” (Milton, IX. 853-877).

This passage is where the relationships between Satan, Eve, and Adam converge, and where Milton's focus shifts from Satan and Eve to Adam and Eve. Satan is not mentioned in the passage and never speaks to Adam at all, having left at 784-85 immediately after Eve tastes the fruit. Yet he still tempts Adam into eating, through Eve. Eve relies here on her own reasoning rather than on God's authority, and the evidence she offers Adam is entirely internal, “Dim erst, dilated spirits, ampler heart” (876), which means Adam is asked to accept a claim about the physical world on the strength of states no one can observe. His acceptance is what turns the Fall into an event instead of Eve's account of one. This shift begins before Eve speaks a single word.

When Eve starts to speak, Milton reports that in her face “came prologue and apology too prompt” (854). Milton uses the excuse as a prologue, meaning that Eve had something ‘scripted’ to say before any argument was made. Milton says she speaks those bland words "at will" (855), which claims she originates what follows, and Milton lets both claims stand without reconciling them: the excuse was ready before the argument was made, and Eve originates what she says.

When Eve describes what God said, she never says that God said it. She refers to the prohibition twice, and both times the speaker vanishes: "as we are told" (863) and "as we are threatened" (870). Normally, warnings have the authority of whoever gave them, yet God’s command to Eve is something that was just told to her. The serpent is the complete opposite, since "Hath eaten of the fruit and is become" (869) gives the serpent real action and authority. Eve doesn’t say that God was wrong, but rather puts the serpent where God used to be.

Eve's reasoning is careful, and Milton makes it careful on purpose. She considers more than one explanation: "The serpent wise, Or not restrained as we or not obeying" (867–68). A disjunction like this is what a mind produces when it acknowledges it might be wrong. Yet the possibilities she lists leave out the ones that matter, since she never considers that the serpent did not eat. The serpent is both her only evidence and the source of that evidence, which is the trap she cannot see. Eve does not fall by failing to think, but by what she accepts as proof.

The proof Eve offers is entirely internal. She claims to have "found Th' effects to correspond" (874-75), but the effects she names are "opener mine eyes, Dim erst, dilated spirits, ampler heart" (875-76), and not one of them could be seen by anyone else. Eve has no way to check them either. She can feel the effects, yet she has nothing outside herself to measure them against, which is exactly why she needed the serpent's case to begin with. Her own experience cannot verify itself. It is also a repetition rather than a test. The serpent promised "to open eyes" (866), and her result is "opener mine eyes" (875), so the correspondence she reports is verbal rather than empirical. This unverifiable evidence is all Adam receives.

Adam is given testimony and nothing else. Eve delivers this testimony using Satan's method, opening with a rhetorical question, "Hast thou not wondered, Adam, at my stay?" (856), which is the same device Satan used on her throughout Book IX. She has acquired his procedure along with his conclusion, and she cannot see that she has. This is how Satan tempts Adam without ever meeting him.

Milton needs Eve's consciousness to have originated something here, because if her deliberation only carries forward a chain running back through Satan, then God becomes the author of the Fall and the poem's defense of him fails. Yet the same passage shows her consciousness reasoning with materials it did not choose and cannot inspect. This is also the one moment in Book IX where a mind visibly produces an effect outside itself, since Eve's private and unverifiable states become speech, and that speech changes Adam's body. Milton shows a mind that looks like it is beginning something at the same time it seems it is continuing something else. If the serpent gave Eve everything she reasons from, did her consciousness start the Fall or just carry it? And if the serpent shaped how Eve thinks, is the thinking still hers or his?

Fruit 02 · Build 2

Individual paragraph

In Paradise Lost (IX.853-877), Eve tries to persuade Adam to eat the fruit, and Milton writes that “excuse / Came prologue, and apology to prompt,” with her excuse for Adam coming before she speaks to him. But, Milton says she speaks “at will,” so Eve experiences the reasoning as her own. As Jaynes says, “we cannot be conscious of what we are not conscious of.” If we never see a thought arrive, then where does it come from? Throughout Westworld, Dolores believes that a voice in her head, Arnold, is telling her what to do; yet only in the finale does she realize that she couldn’t have been speaking to Arnold and that the voice was her own thoughts. This realization is where consciousness begins for Jaynes. Eve never reaches that point of consciousness because she never questions her thoughts and trusts her own reason instead of God’s authority.

Fruit 03 · Build 3

Build Three

In Build 1, I treated Eve's reasoning as something that began when Satan spoke to her, but my second reading changed that. In Book IV, Satan imagines repenting but doesn’t because he has already decided that his future self will repeat the past and won’t deliberate. In Book IX, Eve reframes Adam’s warning before she ever meets the serpent. Both choose what a moment means to protect who they believe they are. When each of them changes, they reconsolidate: altering their description of events so it fits who they have become. The mind does not just record what happens; it keeps rewriting who it is. Satan, Eve, and Adam each survive a change in who they are by reconsolidating, and what separates them is not whether they rewrite their continuity but who supplies and influences the words they use.

What happens when no one gives you the words? Satan reconsolidates without anyone's help. In Eden he says “the more I see / Pleasures about me so much more I feel / Torment within me” (IX.119-121). Normally, seeing something so beautiful as Eden would make a person feel better, yet Satan feels worse. Eden does not change, but Satan does. Scarry argues that people put their feelings into objects so those objects can ease their pain. Satan does the opposite and projects his pain onto Eden, so the more of Eden he sees, the more it becomes part of what he is stuck with. He rewrites the world around him to fit the fallen self he chose. This led me to Eve, who reconsolidates almost without noticing.

When Adam warns Eve that Satan may deceive her, she answers that Adam must not trust her. She turns this into a statement about herself. Eve insists to Adam that she is strong enough to be alone, while also leaving Adam stuck because any additional warning would just prove her point. She changes what Adam’s words mean so they fit the self she wants to be. Adam warns that reason can “dictate false and misinform the will” (IX.355), and since Eve cannot check her own reasoning, she can rewrite what things mean without noticing, exactly as Adam predicted. Even before eating the fruit, Eve is reconsolidating. Satan isn’t teaching her how to change but is just giving her new words to do it with.

Satan gives Eve new words before he gives her the fruit. He appeals to Eve’s pride by telling her that she’s “a goddess among gods” (IX.547). After eating, Eve repeats this idea to Adam, saying that she is “growing up to godhead” (IX.877), meaning that she’s turning into a god herself, just as Satan said. Satan also gives her new words for God’s command. He says her knowledge could not hurt God “if all be His,” then asks, “Or is it envy? And can envy dwell / In Heav’nly breasts?” (IX.728-730). If everything belongs to God, envy makes little sense. Yet the question still changes what the command means to Eve, turning protection into jealousy. Eve’s reconsolidation now runs on Satan’s words, so the self she builds is one he described first. With Satan’s words, Eve rewrites her future, and does the same to the tree.

Eve also tells Adam that “This tree is not as we are told a tree / Of danger” (IX.863-864). Just like Satan’s view of Eden, nothing about the tree has changed, only how Eve describes it. Like the Ship of Theseus: is the tree different when nothing about it physically changes, but its description does? Eve does to herself what she did to the tree, changing the description but not the reality. Satan promised that “your eyes…shall perfectly be then / Opened and cleared,” (IX.706-708) and while they do, they find “their minds / How darkened” (IX.1053-1054). Her reconsolidation changes the story, but not what happens to her. It might seem that Eve’s only problem is that she used Satan’s words, but Adam uses his own words and ends up just as hopeless.

After the fall, Adam puts the blame “on me, me only” (X.832). His own words lead him where Satan led: “crime and doom” (X.841). Satan and Adam both use their own words and end up hopeless, while Eve borrows Satan’s words and falls. I began by thinking the source of the words decided everything, but Adam made me doubt that. If neither one’s own words nor someone else’s protect someone, does reconsolidation ever help? And if Eve’s tree becomes something new without changing, is Eve the same as she leaves Eden, or just redescribed?

Fruit 04 · Build 4

Build Four

In Build 3, I argued that the mind does not record what happens; it continually rewrites who it is. I called this process reconsolidation: revising the story of one's past, present, or future so that it fits the person one has become. One might object, however, that reconsolidation does not fit Milton's conception of consciousness, because in Paradise Lost the mind cannot escape an honest record of itself. When Satan is alone in Book IV, his memory turns against him: "conscience wakes despair / That slumbered, wakes the bitter memory / Of what he was, what is, and what must be" (IV.23-25). This line mentions the past, present, and future, all parts of what reconsolidation is supposed to change, yet Satan’s conscience gives him no choice about which of them to keep. The Fall was freely chosen, and a character can only be held responsible for a choice if the mind preserves an accurate record of it. If Satan could truly rewrite who he is, there would be no one left to hold responsible for what he did.

The same soliloquy that preserves Satan’s honest record also shows him rewriting what that record means. He asks himself whether he had the same opportunity to remain loyal as every other angel: "Hadst thou the same free will and pow'r to stand? / Thou hadst" (IV.66-67). He concedes the truth that condemns him. Yet the same speech ends with a decision about what that truth will mean: "Evil, be thou my good" (IV.110). Satan’s past has not changed; only its significance has. A fall that he openly admits was his own fault becomes, by the end of the speech, the foundation of a new identity. Satan cannot rewrite the facts of his past, so he rewrites what those facts mean for who he is. The record and the reconsolidation are not competing accounts of his mind; the record is the material that reconsolidation works on.

Adam demonstrates that this process continues even when reason is functioning honestly. After the Fall, his reasoning leads him directly to his own guilt, and he first expresses it as total responsibility: "On me, me only" (X.832). Within a few lines, however, the same guilt becomes a burden "divided / With that bad woman" (X.836-37). Adam learns nothing new between these two statements. The facts remain fixed, while the story he tells about them swings from sole shared blame. His mind is accurate about what happened, but it continues searching for a version of himself that he can bear to be.

Eve shows most clearly how the honest record and the rewritten meaning can exist side by side in one mind. Preparing to return to Adam, she weighs how to present “my change” (IX.818), so she knows something has happened; her record is intact. What she revises is what that change means for who she is. When she reaches Adam, she claims an “ampler heart” (IX.876), meaning a braver one. In her account, eating the fruit was not disobedience but the moment she became braver. Satan supplied this interpretation when he praised her “dauntless virtue” (IX.694), since “virtue” meant both goodness and daring, but Eve adopts it as her own. The event remains fixed; only its meaning has shifted, from a fall to a kind of growth. And Eve’s revision does not stay inside her mind, because the meaning she gives her change soon becomes a reason to act.

However, if one considers how Milton uses reconsolidation in order to turn a past that cannot be changed into a decision about what to do next, one can begin to see how reconsolidation might allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality to influence its course. The record itself never moves: Satan still fell, and Eve still ate. Yet each time a character revises what the record means, that new meaning directs a new action. Once Eve has decided what her change signifies, she resolves that "Adam shall share with me in bliss or woe" (IX.831) and carries the fruit back to him, a physical act that brings about a second fall. Consciousness cannot change what has happened, but by reinterpreting it, it chooses how events play out. I think this answers the objection’s concern about responsibility: reconsolidation makes Milton’s characters more responsible, not less. Because they revise their stories with the truth in full view, each new meaning is a choice, not a mistake. But if the Fall has already altered the self that rewrites, how free can that choice be?

Fruit 05 · Build 5

Build Five

In Build 4, I argued that the mind keeps an honest record of what happens but continually rewrites what that record means for who it is. I call this process reconsolidation: revising the story of one’s past, present, or future so that it fits the person one has become. Like Milton's Satan, Descartes reasons alone, answering a crisis of self-understanding by rewriting his past and naming the self that emerges. Although they use reconsolidation differently, comparing them reveals what neither shows alone: the mind can only rewrite itself by leaning on a part of itself that it cannot rewrite.

However, a crucial difference lies in how each mind treats a story that flatters it, the pleasant story it would rather believe than test. Descartes compares himself to a prisoner who dreams he is free and "goes along with the pleasant illusion as long as he can" (Descartes, 15). The dream flatters because it offers a freedom he is denied. Yet, he resolves to “guard against assenting to any falsehoods” (15). This “assenting” shows how no new account of the self takes hold without consent earned through doubt. Eve also faces a deceiver offering a flattering story, but she grants her assent freely. Satan praises her "dauntless virtue" (Milton, IX.694), and after eating she claims an "ampler heart" (IX.876) without questioning its source. Descartes tests each revision against the truth, while Eve accepts whichever revision lets her see her fall as growth.

Descartes resists a flattering story that Eve accepts because of each mind’s materials. Descartes's one certainty has no history attached to it and only lasts for “for as long as I am thinking," (18) so he must build continuity deliberately, pausing to “to fix it more deeply in my memory" (23). Satan's situation is the reverse: "wakes the bitter memory / Of what he was, what is, and what must be" (IV.24-25). Descartes acts on memory, while memory acts on Satan. For Descartes, continuity is an achievement that reconsolidation constructs. For Milton, it is already given and reconsolidation can only reinterpret it. Satan remembers everything he was, and that awareness is his punishment.

Yet, beneath these differences lies a common insight, namely that reconsolidation depends on a part of the self that cannot itself be revised. Descartes recalls that Archimedes demanded “one firm and immovable point in order to shift the entire earth" (16). His "I am" becomes that point, and every later revision of who he is pushes off it. Satan discovers the same structure as a curse: "Which way I fly is Hell, myself am Hell" (IV.75). He can rename his good and evil, but he cannot escape the self that does the renaming. In Build 4, I treated the honest record as a limit that pushes back against reconsolidation. These texts suggest it is also a fulcrum, the one place the mind can stand while it moves everything else. Still, Descartes finds his “I am” only after doubting everything, so reconsolidation may uncover its fixed point rather than simply stand on it.

Milton and Descartes both use reconsolidation in order to change who the self is by pushing off a fixed point the mind cannot revise. Yet, how can reconsolidation allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality and influence its course? A physical cause fixes what happens, but not what it means. The same bite can mean a fall or growth, and nothing in the fruit decides between them. Descartes imagines a demon who controls everything he perceives, even his own body, yet who "will be unable to impose on me in the slightest degree" (15). If an all-powerful cause can shape every appearance but not the mind's yes or no, assent is not simply the chain's next link. Since that yes or no comes after the event, the present decides what the past causes next: once Eve decides her fall was growth, she carries the fruit back to Adam, and her new meaning becomes a physical act. If consciousness adds no new force but only steers the chain from its fulcrum, is it the hand that pushes the lever, or only the lever itself?
