Build Six

How does a mind outlive the self it was? In Paradise Lost, Milton answers in reconsolidation: Satan, Eve, and Adam each respond to their fall by rewriting what their past means. Yet the record resists. Satan’s conscience still “wakes the bitter memory” of what he was (IV.24), so the mind can reinterpret its history but never erase it. Descartes puts that limit to use. If even doubt cannot shake Descartes’s certainty that he exists, then he can use that one fixed point to rebuild everything else he believes. Whether that fulcrum becomes a foundation or a prison, and whether the will acts freely at all, may depend on which way consciousness chooses to push.

Milton’s characters, conceivably, push in both directions. Satan and Adam face the same fall, yet Satan decides it proves he will always rebel, while Adam sees something to make right and repents. Eve exposes the limit of this freedom, since she gives Adam different reasons for eating the fruit than the ones that moved her, and Milton calls that an excuse. Montaigne adds another complication, arguing that how we see ourselves depends on the moment we look. If so, reconsolidation probably frees us only when we read the past honestly and notice how the present colors it.

Honest reading, though, proves difficult. Consider how Adam and Eve react after eating the forbidden fruit. Milton traces how narration allows them to connect past events with their present feelings and imagined futures. Yet narration does not always lead to a better understanding of their actions. Adam and Eve instead begin using their versions of events to justify themselves and avoid responsibility. This suggests that narration can both help and limit consciousness, depending on the purpose behind it. Ultimately, making sense of our experiences perhaps does not mean understanding them truthfully or accepting responsibility.

Satan carries this self-justification furthest. Satan and Eve first taught us reconsolidation as constructing one’s past, present, and future into one continuous sense of self, with Satan’s “Evil, be thou my good” (IV.110) explicitly completing this process. Upon scrutinizing this understanding, we came to acknowledge that reconsolidation and rationalization function symbiotically, suggesting that consciousness may consist in the capacity to develop a continuous identity, even through subjective truth. After comparing Paradise Lost to the Bhagavad Gita, we arrived at our current understanding. Reconsolidation takes many different forms in people, but what one chooses to relinquish or renounce seems ultimately to decide how one reconsolidates.

While reconsolidation explains how the self takes shape, it leaves open how the self comes to examine its own mind. We first defined reflexivity as unique to each character yet similarly harmful, since it highlighted the irrationality and emotional influences behind each character’s choices. We then realized that reflexivity carries no inherent harm; each character handles self-awareness differently, and some find positive uses for it. We claimed that moments of crisis catalyze self-awareness that leads to conscious action. Pain, perhaps the sharpest of these crises, led us to Scarry, who, like Milton, presents it as a catalyst for reflexive consciousness and ties consciousness directly to understanding others, or empathy.

Yet even if pain prompts reflection, the question remains whether that reflection actually changes as a result. Throughout our examination of the reading, we explored how reflexivity influences the relationship between consciousness and subsequent action. We focused on how Eve’s desires shape the way she reasons about her decisions. As we developed this idea, we began to question whether her reasoning illustrates genuine reflexivity or whether she simply rationalizes choices she already wants to make. Comparing Milton with the Bhagavad Gita allowed us to visualize this idea from another perspective. Krishna’s teachings about desire, self-understanding, and action led us to consider how reflexive thought might allow us not only to recognize our impulses, but to respond to them differently, perhaps the point at which free will begins.
