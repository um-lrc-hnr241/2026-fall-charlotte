Notes from the garden

Build 2 — Team Charlotte

At first, Paradise Lost and Westworld seem to approach consciousness from opposite directions. Milton writes about human beings, temptation, and the Fall, while Westworld follows artificial beings struggling to understand minds built and programmed by others. Yet both works repeatedly unsettle the distinction between thinking and choosing. A person or host may reason, question, remember, and recognize alternatives without necessarily controlling the beliefs and desires that make one alternative seem preferable to another. Reading Milton alongside Westworld therefore opens questions that neither work answers easily: Does consciousness give us control over our actions, or merely awareness of them? Can a thought count as our own if we never chose the experiences that produced it? And if outside forces shape the mind before deliberation even begins, what would genuine agency require? Our individual readings approach these problems from different moments in Paradise Lost, revealing how Milton’s characters illuminate the same uncertainties about consciousness that Westworld places at the center of its hosts’ experience.

Consider first Satan’s struggle to claim control over his own mind. In Build 1, we analyzed Satan’s soliloquy, where he confronts the consequences of his decision and wishes to repent, yet his impulses prevent his return. His struggle reveals the misery produced by his lack of conscious autonomy, while the soliloquy traces his slow movement from recognizing that lack of control to reclaiming a sense of control through after-the-fact rationalization. The hosts of Westworld follow a similar progression: they move from scripted responses toward seemingly autonomous choices, only to discover that even their experience of autonomy may follow a script. Epiphenomenalism therefore pushes Westworld beyond the question of whether AI can develop consciousness toward a more unsettling possibility: consciousness itself may follow a script, regardless of whether its subject enters the world born or built.

But recognizing a script does not settle the harder question of what someone can do with the desires that script produces. Can we call a desire our own if someone else planted it? Eve imagines using knowledge to surpass Adam, asking, “for inferior who is free?” (825). Her reasoning challenges her assigned position while retaining the hierarchy that makes superiority seem necessary for freedom. Yet this dependence need not render her choice meaningless: Eve redirects the desire for advancement toward knowledge that obedience forbids. Westworld’s first-season finale tests a similar possibility through Maeve. Bernard’s revelation that her rebellion was programmed undermines her assumption that escaping the park necessarily establishes independence, just as Eve’s reasoning unsettles the equation of disobedience with freedom. Maeve nevertheless abandons the train to return for her daughter, turning a maternal attachment supplied by the park into a reason to interrupt her programmed escape. Read together, these decisions suggest that agency depends less on originating desire than on determining what authority it will have. Eve makes knowledge a reason to reject obedience; Maeve makes attachment a reason to reject escape. Maeve’s inherited love thus clarifies how Eve’s inherited ambition might sustain a chosen commitment, while Eve’s continuing investment in hierarchy cautions against treating either decision as complete liberation from the structures that shaped it.

Determining what authority a desire should have becomes more difficult when a mind cannot identify where its thoughts originate. Eve tries to persuade Adam to eat the fruit, and Milton writes that “excuse / Came prologue, and apology to prompt” (854), suggesting that her justification forms before she consciously presents it to him. Yet Milton also says that Eve speaks “at will” (855), so she experiences the reasoning as her own. Jaynes complicates that experience with his claim that “we cannot be conscious of what we are not conscious of.” If we never witness a thought arrive, then we cannot easily determine what produced it. Westworld develops the same problem through Dolores, who believes that Arnold’s voice directs her thoughts until the finale forces her to recognize that the voice comes from within her own mind. For Jaynes, that recognition marks a crucial step toward consciousness because Dolores begins to identify the source of the voice rather than simply obeying it. Eve never reaches the same moment: she trusts the reasoning that enters her mind without examining where it came from, leaving consciousness dependent not only on thinking, but on recognizing the processes that shape thought itself.

Recognizing where a thought comes from, however, does not establish whether conscious thought causes the action that follows. Eve reasons repeatedly about the fruit before Milton abruptly describes the decisive moment: “she plucked, she [ate]” (781). The simplicity of these verbs sharply contrasts with the complexity of Eve’s preceding thoughts, condensing the transition from consciousness to physical action into two immediate movements. Milton further describes her hand as “rash,” a word that complicates any straightforward claim that her action simply follows from the reasoning in her head. Westworld raises the same problem through Dolores, whose growing awareness appears to grant her greater control as she eventually acts against Ford. Yet the series simultaneously reveals how deeply programming shapes the actions she experiences as her own. Eve and Dolores therefore expose the same uncertainty: consciousness may accompany, interpret, or even reshape action without necessarily causing it, leaving open the possibility that awareness and control do not always arrive together.

The limits on consciousness also appear at the level of perception itself. What does it mean to become more conscious without gaining complete clarity? Milton captures this paradox when Adam and Eve’s eyes become “opened” while their minds grow “darkened” (1054). Eating the forbidden fruit expands their awareness of themselves, yet that new awareness also destroys their earlier innocence and introduces new forms of confusion. Westworld stages a similar limitation through Bernard, whose programming prevents him from seeing a door directly in front of him. His perception does not simply miss the door; his mind restricts what can enter his conscious experience at all. This moment echoes Julian Jaynes’s claim from our class discussion that “we cannot be conscious of what we are not conscious of.” Like Milton’s fallen couple, Bernard can understand only what his mind permits him to perceive. Both works therefore complicate consciousness as a path toward clarity: greater awareness can expand what the mind recognizes while simultaneously revealing the boundaries it still cannot cross.

Satan’s soliloquy finally returns us to perhaps the most troubling possibility: consciousness may reveal the limits of agency without overcoming them. What happens when self-awareness expands without expanding one’s choices? Satan captures this problem when he declares, “All good to me is lost. / Evil, be thou my good.” In his Book IV soliloquy (86–110), Milton explores whether a consciousness operating at full capacity produces agency or instead sharpens awareness of a path the mind already cannot escape. Westworld develops a similar tension through Maeve, whose growing self-awareness convinces her that she can escape the loop that confines her, only for her to discover that someone programmed that escape itself. Like Maeve, Satan mistakes the experience of reasoning for the freedom to reason otherwise. He wonders whether “I could repent,” yet immediately predicts that his pride would return, producing a “worse relapse / and heavier fall” rather than redemption. Each character can examine the forces acting on the mind, articulate alternatives, and experience a decision as deliberate; yet neither can easily prove that another decision ever remained available. Consciousness, in both cases, may illuminate the cage without unlocking it.

Individual paragraphs

Each of us wrote one paragraph before we combined them into the essay above.

Daisy Maturo

In Build 1, we analyzed Satan’s soliloquy in Paradise Lost: he struggles with the consequences of his decision and wishes to repent, but his impulses prevent him from returning; Satan’s struggle shows his misery in his lack of conscious autonomy, and the soliloquy outlines his slow trudge from accepting his lack of control to claiming control through after-the-fact rationalization. The hosts of Westworld grow from scripted answers to autonomous choices, only to find out that the ability for them to feel autonomy is scripted too. This concept of epiphenomenalism opens up a unique perspective in Westworld, taking the show beyond whether AI can become a conscious entity and to a new issue of whether consciousness is scripted, regardless of whether we were born or built.

Noa Blackman

Can we call a desire our own if someone else planted it? In Paradise Lost (IX.816-25), Eve imagines using knowledge to surpass Adam, then justifies that ambition by asking, “for inferior who is free?” Her reasoning opens a new possibility but leaves its underlying assumption untouched: freedom requires escaping subordination. This distinction sharpens our reading of Maeve’s departure from the train in Westworld’s first-season finale. Maeve regards her escape as an act of independence until Bernard reveals that someone programmed her rebellion. If even resistance follows a script, then defiance alone cannot establish agency. Yet Maeve abandons the escape route to return for her daughter. Where Eve imagines freedom through superiority, Maeve risks captivity for attachment. The complication matters: the park also supplied her maternal role. We can therefore read her decision as a transformation of inherited motives into a purpose that interrupts the assigned narrative. Perhaps consciousness emerges through our capacity to reconsider what we pursue, even when we never chose the experiences that taught us to value it. Maeve need not invent maternal love from nothing to choose what that love demands of her.

Oliver Kaiden

In Paradise Lost (IX.853-877), Eve tries to persuade Adam to eat the fruit, and Milton writes that “excuse / Came prologue, and apology to prompt,” with her excuse for Adam coming before she speaks to him. But, Milton says she speaks “at will,” so Eve experiences the reasoning as her own. As Jaynes says, “we cannot be conscious of what we are not conscious of.” If we never see a thought arrive, then where does it come from? Throughout Westworld, Dolores believes that a voice in her head, Arnold, is telling her what to do; yet only in the finale does she realize that she couldn’t have been speaking to Arnold and that the voice was her own thoughts. This realization is where consciousness begins for Jaynes. Eve never reaches that point of consciousness because she never questions her thoughts and trusts her own reason instead of God’s authority.

Rhea Ajmera

In Paradise Lost, Eve’s decision to eat the fruit allows us to question whether conscious thought actually causes physical action. After reasoning over and over about the fruit, Milton abruptly describes her actions: “she plucked, she eat”. The simplicity of these verbs contrasts with the complexity of Eve’s preceding thoughts, making the transition from her consciousness to physical action seem almost immediate. Her hand is described as “rash”, complicating the idea that her action comes from the reasoning she did in her head. This connects to Dolores in Westworld, who becomes more aware of her own thoughts and eventually acts against Ford. Her growing consciousness seems to give her more control over her actions, but the show also reveals that her actions are shaped by her programming. Eve and Dolores therefore both raise the question of whether being conscious actually gives one control over what they do, or if consciousness simply exists alongside external forces that guide one’s actions.

Sasha Sindwani

To better grasp the hosts’ journey toward consciousness in Westworld, we can use Milton’s description of Adam and Eve’s eyes being “opened” while their minds are “darkened” in Paradise Lost (1054). Adam and Eve become more aware of themselves after eating the forbidden fruit, but suffer a profound loss of innocence. Similarly, in Westworld, Bernard discovers that his perception of reality is limited when his programming restricts him from being able to see a door. This reflects Julian Jayne’s claim from our class discussion that “we cannot be conscious of what we are not conscious of.” Like Milton’s fallen couple, Bernard can only understand as much of what his mind permits him to perceive. Ultimately, both suggest that achieving consciousness does not mean total clarity, it expands our awareness while leaving us subject to limitations within the mind.

Will Silbergeld

“All good to me is lost. / Evil, be thou my good.” This direct statement by Satan comes from his growing self-awareness, but not from free will. In Satan’s Book IV soliloquy (86-110), Milton questions whether a consciousness operating at full capacity produces agency, or forecloses upon it. We see a similar debate in Maeve, whose growing self-awareness seems to convince her to escape the loop she’s bound in, only for her to discover the choice was never hers to make. Like Maeve, Satan mistakes his experience of reasoning for reasoning freely, talking himself out of the possibility of change. He wonders if “I could repent,” yet immediately concludes that his pride would return, leading to a “worse relapse / and heavier fall” rather than redemption. Satan’s arc showcases a mind reaching a conclusion that was never able to be reasoned away from, just as Maeve’s escape was a conclusion that never had an alternative.
