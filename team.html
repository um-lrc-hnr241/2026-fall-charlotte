Six paths

Six members, the Westworld character each of us took on, our roles, and the work each of us contributed.

SS

Sasha Sindwani

Ford · Recorder

Fruit 02 · Build 2 paragraph

To better grasp the hosts’ journey toward consciousness in Westworld, we can use Milton’s description of Adam and Eve’s eyes being “opened” while their minds are “darkened” in Paradise Lost (1054). Adam and Eve become more aware of themselves after eating the forbidden fruit, but suffer a profound loss of innocence. Similarly, in Westworld, Bernard discovers that his perception of reality is limited when his programming restricts him from being able to see a door. This reflects Julian Jayne’s claim from our class discussion that “we cannot be conscious of what we are not conscious of.” Like Milton’s fallen couple, Bernard can only understand as much of what his mind permits him to perceive. Ultimately, both suggest that achieving consciousness does not mean total clarity, it expands our awareness while leaving us subject to limitations within the mind.

NB

Noa Blackman

Charlotte · Spokesperson

Fruit 02 · Build 2 paragraph

Can we call a desire our own if someone else planted it? In Paradise Lost (IX.816-25), Eve imagines using knowledge to surpass Adam, then justifies that ambition by asking, “for inferior who is free?” Her reasoning opens a new possibility but leaves its underlying assumption untouched: freedom requires escaping subordination. This distinction sharpens our reading of Maeve’s departure from the train in Westworld’s first-season finale. Maeve regards her escape as an act of independence until Bernard reveals that someone programmed her rebellion. If even resistance follows a script, then defiance alone cannot establish agency. Yet Maeve abandons the escape route to return for her daughter. Where Eve imagines freedom through superiority, Maeve risks captivity for attachment. The complication matters: the park also supplied her maternal role. We can therefore read her decision as a transformation of inherited motives into a purpose that interrupts the assigned narrative. Perhaps consciousness emerges through our capacity to reconsider what we pursue, even when we never chose the experiences that taught us to value it. Maeve need not invent maternal love from nothing to choose what that love demands of her.

OK

Ollie Kaiden

Bernard · Moderator

Fruit 02 · Build 2 paragraph

In Paradise Lost (IX.853-877), Eve tries to persuade Adam to eat the fruit, and Milton writes that “excuse / Came prologue, and apology to prompt,” with her excuse for Adam coming before she speaks to him. But, Milton says she speaks “at will,” so Eve experiences the reasoning as her own. As Jaynes says, “we cannot be conscious of what we are not conscious of.” If we never see a thought arrive, then where does it come from? Throughout Westworld, Dolores believes that a voice in her head, Arnold, is telling her what to do; yet only in the finale does she realize that she couldn’t have been speaking to Arnold and that the voice was her own thoughts. This realization is where consciousness begins for Jaynes. Eve never reaches that point of consciousness because she never questions her thoughts and trusts her own reason instead of God’s authority.

RA

Rhea Ajmera

Elsie · Checker

Fruit 02 · Build 2 paragraph

In Paradise Lost, Eve’s decision to eat the fruit allows us to question whether conscious thought actually causes physical action. After reasoning over and over about the fruit, Milton abruptly describes her actions: “she plucked, she eat”. The simplicity of these verbs contrasts with the complexity of Eve’s preceding thoughts, making the transition from her consciousness to physical action seem almost immediate. Her hand is described as “rash”, complicating the idea that her action comes from the reasoning she did in her head. This connects to Dolores in Westworld, who becomes more aware of her own thoughts and eventually acts against Ford. Her growing consciousness seems to give her more control over her actions, but the show also reveals that her actions are shaped by her programming. Eve and Dolores therefore both raise the question of whether being conscious actually gives one control over what they do, or if consciousness simply exists alongside external forces that guide one’s actions.

WS

Will Silbergeld

Man-in-Black · Devil’s advocate

Fruit 02 · Build 2 paragraph

“All good to me is lost. / Evil, be thou my good.” This direct statement by Satan comes from his growing self-awareness, but not from free will. In Satan’s Book IV soliloquy (86-110), Milton questions whether a consciousness operating at full capacity produces agency, or forecloses upon it. We see a similar debate in Maeve, whose growing self-awareness seems to convince her to escape the loop she’s bound in, only for her to discover the choice was never hers to make. Like Maeve, Satan mistakes his experience of reasoning for reasoning freely, talking himself out of the possibility of change. He wonders if “I could repent,” yet immediately concludes that his pride would return, leading to a “worse relapse / and heavier fall” rather than redemption. Satan’s arc showcases a mind reaching a conclusion that was never able to be reasoned away from, just as Maeve’s escape was a conclusion that never had an alternative.

DM

Daisy Maturo

Arnold · Designer

Fruit 02 · Build 2 paragraph

In Build 1, we analyzed Satan’s soliloquy in Paradise Lost: he struggles with the consequences of his decision and wishes to repent, but his impulses prevent him from returning; Satan’s struggle shows his misery in his lack of conscious autonomy, and the soliloquy outlines his slow trudge from accepting his lack of control to claiming control through after-the-fact rationalization. The hosts of Westworld grow from scripted answers to autonomous choices, only to find out that the ability for them to feel autonomy is scripted too. This concept of epiphenomenalism opens up a unique perspective in Westworld, taking the show beyond whether AI can become a conscious entity and to a new issue of whether consciousness is scripted, regardless of whether we were born or built.
